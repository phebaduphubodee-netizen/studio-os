### 1. Duvet Tog Rating to Fill Weight (g/m²) and Thickness (mm)

The relationship between tog rating, fill weight (GSM or g/m²), and thickness is not a direct linear conversion. Tog is a [MEASURED] unit of thermal resistance (1 tog = 0.1 m²K/W) defined by standards like BS 5335 and tested via methods in BS 4745. Thickness and fill weight are contributing factors, but the material's efficiency at trapping air (its thermal conductivity) is the primary determinant. Natural fills like down are often lighter and less thick than synthetic fills for the same tog rating because they insulate more efficiently.

### 4. Bedding Drop/Overhang Conventions

NO SOURCED VALUE FOUND for formal standards. The following are [PRACTICE] conventions from interior design and retail guides. The drop depends on the desired style and the height of the mattress and bed frame.

*   **Comforter/Duvet:** The goal is typically to cover the mattress and the top of the box spring or bed frame. For modern platform beds, this may mean just covering the mattress.
    *   **Typical Drop:** A common recommendation is for the duvet to hang down far enough to cover the sides of the-mattress, often with an additional few inches. For a 250-350 mm (10-14 inch) mattress, this implies a drop of **300–450 mm (12–18 inches)** on each side.
*   **Coverlet:** Designed to be lighter and often used as a decorative layer. It typically has a shorter drop than a comforter.
    *   **Typical Drop:** Designed to cover the mattress and meet the top of a bed skirt or frame. For a 250-350 mm mattress, this suggests a drop of **250–380 mm (10–15 inches)**.
*   **Bedspread:** The largest of the three, designed to cover the entire bed, including the pillows, and drop all the way to the floor.
    *   **Typical Drop:** The drop is calculated based on the height from the top of the mattress to the floor. For a typical bed height of 600-700 mm, and a 300 mm mattress, the drop would be approximately **600 mm (24 inches)**.

These are styling conventions, not manufacturing standards, and vary widely.

[INFERENCE] The behaviour can be described qualitatively based on common knowledge of the materials:
*   **Down/Feather:** Will show significant initial indentation with a soft, enveloping feel. High-quality down will have more "spring" and resist bottoming out.

### 6. Areal Weights (g/m²) and Drape/Stiffness for Bedding Fabrics

Finding sourced, paired values for both areal weight and bending/drape for specific, named bedding fabrics is difficult. However, typical weights and the standards for measuring drape are available.

**Areal Weights (g/m²):**
These are common [PRACTICE] values. Thread count (TC) is often used as a proxy for quality, but g/m² is the direct measure of mass.
*   **Cotton Percale:** Typically ranges from **100 to 130 g/m²**. (Often described as light and crisp).
*   **Cotton Sateen:** Typically ranges from **110 to 150 g/m²**. (Often described as heavier and silkier than percale).
*   **Linen:** Highly variable, but bedding linen typically ranges from **150 to 200 g/m²**.
*   **Brushed Cotton (Flannel):** Typically starts around **140 g/m²** and can go up to **170 g/m²** or more.

**Bending Rigidity / Drape Coefficient:**
These properties are [MEASURED] using standardized tests.
*   **Test Standards:**
    *   **Bending Stiffness:** ASTM D1388 (Standard Test Method for Stiffness of Fabrics) is a common standard using the Cantilever test. Results are given as "bending length" or "flexural rigidity".
    *   **Drape:** The "Cusick Drape Test" is a well-known method that measures the area of a circular fabric specimen as it hangs over a smaller circular disk. The result is the "drape coefficient," a dimensionless value (often expressed as a percentage) where a lower value means the fabric is more pliable and drapes more.
*   **Published Values:** NO SOURCED VALUE FOUND for a comprehensive table of these specific bedding fabrics with their corresponding drape coefficients or bending lengths. These tests are typically performed by textile labs for specific commercial products and the results are not widely published in a reference database.

**Mapping to Cloth Solvers:**
This is a [PRACTICE] convention, and the mapping is often non-linear and requires artistic adjustment. There is no universally accepted direct formula.
*   **Source:** The Blender 3.3 Manual provides definitions for its cloth parameters.
*   **Procedure [PRACTICE]:**
    1.  **Mass:** The solver's `Vertex Mass` parameter is directly related to areal weight. The total mass of the cloth object should equal the real-world object's mass. This can be set by taking the fabric's g/m², multiplying by the object's surface area in m², and distributing that mass across the vertices. Blender's `Mass` setting in the `Physical Properties` tab is a multiplier, but the core value is set under `Vertex Mass`.
    2.  **Bending Stiffness:** The solver's `Bending` parameter in the `Stiffness` section controls resistance to folding. This conceptually maps to the results of a test like ASTM D1388. A higher bending stiffness value in the test would correspond to a higher `Bending` value in the solver. The relationship is not 1:1 with the physical measurement; it is set by eye to match observed behaviour.
    3.  **Drape:** The drape coefficient is a result of multiple physical properties, primarily bending stiffness and weight. In a solver, a fabric with low `Bending` stiffness and appropriate `Vertex Mass` will produce a low drape coefficient (many small, soft folds). A fabric with high `Bending` stiffness will produce a high drape coefficient (fewer, broader, stiffer folds).
(Source: Blender 3.3 Manual, "Cloth Physics Properties")

### 7. Production Cloth-Simulation of Quilted Duvets

This is a summary of [PRACTICE] techniques used by 3D artists, often shared in tutorials and forums.

*   **Loft Representation:**
    *   **Pressure:** The most common method. The duvet is modeled as a closed, airtight mesh "bag". The `Pressure` setting in the cloth physics panel is enabled, and a positive value is used to inflate the object, creating the loft. The `Pressure` value is unitless in Blender and is adjusted visually. Higher values create a tauter, more inflated look. [PRACTICE] (Source: Common knowledge from Blender community tutorials).
    *   **Internal Springs:** An older or alternative method involves creating an internal structure of vertices or edges and enabling `Internal Springs`. This can give more control over the internal structure but is often more complex to set up than pressure. [PRACTICE] (Source: Blender Manual, "Cloth Internal Springs").
*   **Shell Thickness:** The visual thickness of the duvet shell is often handled by a `Solidify` modifier applied *after* the Cloth modifier in the modifier stack. The cloth simulation runs on a single-layer plane or shell, and the final thickness is added non-destructively. [PRACTICE]
*   **Quilting Seams:**
    *   **Pinning:** The most common method. A vertex group is created containing all the vertices that should be part of the quilted seams. This vertex group is assigned as a `Pin Group` in the cloth settings. The pinned vertices have their simulation goal strength set to 1, effectively locking them to their original animated position while the rest of the cloth simulates around them. [PRACTICE] (Source: Common knowledge from Blender community tutorials).
    *   **Structural Stiffness Groups:** An alternative is to use vertex groups to apply much higher bending stiffness values along the seam lines, causing the fabric to fold preferentially along those lines without fully pinning them. [PRACTICE]
*   **Edge-Roll Formation:** The thick, rolled hem of a duvet is typically modeled geometrically. The simulation mesh will often be a simple sealed bag, and the visual mesh (using a `Surface Deform` modifier bound to the simulation mesh) will have the detailed hem geometry modeled into it. Simulating the hem directly is often too complex and unstable. [PRACTICE]
*   **Parameter Bands:** NO SOURCED VALUE FOUND. Parameter values for pressure, stiffness, damping, etc., are highly dependent on the scene scale, mesh density, and desired artistic effect. They are almost always set empirically by the artist and are not published in standardized bands.
