### 1. Photographers' Use of Lighting Layers in Bedroom Photography

[PRACTICE] Professional interior photographers generally prioritize a primary, soft, motivated light source (often simulating natural light) and frequently turn OFF or selectively use existing artificial lights to avoid conflicting color temperatures and harsh shadows.

*   **Ceiling Downlights (Recessed Cans):** Often turned OFF. [PRACTICE] Architectural photographer Mike Kelley advises turning off interior practical lighting during daylight shoots to avoid the "orange glow" of tungsten lights mixing with the blue of daylight, which creates color chaos and does not represent how people actually live in the space. The goal is a single, consistent color source, which is typically the natural light (or flash simulating it). Other photographers note that overhead lights create harsh, unflattering shadows.
*   **Cove/Coffer Lighting:** Used selectively. [INFERENCE] While not explicitly mentioned in photographer guides as a separate layer, cove lighting would be treated like other practical lights. It would likely be switched off if its color temperature conflicts with the main light source or if the photographer wants to create more dramatic shaping with their own strobes. It might be used in a twilight or evening shot where it becomes the primary ambient source.
*   **Bedside Lamps & Wall Sconces:** Used selectively, often as a "practical" light within the frame. [PRACTICE] The common technique is to have them on to add a sense of warmth and realism, but the photographer will often replace the bulb with a lower-wattage or color-matched (to their strobes) option. The main illumination of the scene comes from the photographer's off-camera flashes, not the practical lamp itself. The goal is for the lamp to *appear* to be lighting the scene, adding a warm glow without actually being the key light source.
*   **Decorative Pendants:** Treated the same as bedside lamps. [PRACTICE] They are typically switched ON for the photograph to show the fixture's design and add a point of interest or "brilliance," but they are not the primary source of illumination for the overall space. Their light is blended in with the main ambient and flash layers.

The stated reason for turning off most practical lights is to gain full control over the color temperature and quality (hardness/softness) of the light in the final image, using off-camera flashes to create a clean, appealing, and often idealized version of the space.

### 2. Dominant Exposure Approach and Luminance Ratios

[PRACTICE] The dominant approach for high-end interior photography is a composite method blending multiple exposures, most commonly known as "flambient" (flash + ambient). This involves layering a natural-light ambient shot with one or more shots where off-camera flashes are used to light specific parts of the room. This provides more control over light and color than single-exposure HDR bracketing.

[PRACTICE] The goal for a visible lamp is to have the shade appear illuminated without "blowing out" (losing all detail to pure white). Photographers achieve this by compositing different exposures: one where the lamp and shade are correctly exposed, and another where the room is correctly exposed.

[NO SOURCED VALUE FOUND] While the technique is widely described, I could not find a specific, practitioner-stated numerical luminance ratio or stop value (e.g., "the lampshade should be 2 stops brighter than the wall"). [INFERENCE] The targeted ratio is an aesthetic judgment made during post-processing, where the opacity of the "lamp on" layer is adjusted until it looks natural and provides a gentle gradient of light on the surrounding wall and surfaces, rather than a clipped, harsh hotspot.

### 3. Manufacturer Cove Lighting Specifications (Lumens & Geometry)

[MEASURED] Manufacturers publish a wide range of lumen outputs for LED strips suitable for residential coves, and provide general geometric guidance.

*   **Lutron:** Offers Lumaris LLD-series LED tape ranging from **200 to 500 lumens per foot** (approx. 656 to 1640 lm/m) for indirect lighting. Their Cove Lighting Application Guide specifies typical geometry:
    *   **Setback (A):** 4 in to 12 in (102 mm to 305 mm) from the wall.
    *   **Fascia Height / Lip (B):** Minimum 1-1/2 in (38 mm) to hide the strip. A common range is 4 in to 6 in (102 mm to 152 mm).
    *   **Distance to Ceiling (C):** 6 in to 18 in (152 mm to 457 mm).
    *   **Gradient Effect:** A smaller setback (e.g., 4 in) creates a shorter, more intense "hot spot" near the edge, while a larger setback (e.g., 12 in) creates a longer, smoother, more even wash across the ceiling.
    *   (Source: Lutron Cove Lighting Application Guide, Document P/N 367-2215 REV B, 2013. Accessed via search, specific document available from Lutron).

*   **WAC Lighting:** Offers InvisiLED Pro series tape ranging from **185 to 430 lumens per foot** (approx. 607 to 1410 lm/m). Their installation instructions provide similar geometric guidance:
    *   **Setback:** A minimum of 4 in (102 mm) is recommended to avoid hot spots.
    *   **Distance to Ceiling:** A minimum of 10 in (254 mm) is recommended for an even wash of light.
    *   (Source: WAC Lighting InvisiLED Pro Installation Instructions, SPEC-IN-L-PRO, 2018).

[INFERENCE] For a typical residential bedroom, the lower end of these ranges (approx. 600-1000 lm/m) is generally sufficient for a pleasant ambient effect, while higher outputs might be used in rooms with very high ceilings.

### 4. Dominant Lighting Layer in Marketing Photography

[PRACTICE] In marketing and real-estate photography, the visually dominant lighting layer is overwhelmingly **bright, soft, natural daylight**, either real or simulated with flash. The goal is to make the space feel airy, large, and welcoming.

[PRACTICE] Professionals often omit ceiling downlights from the shot altogether under the following conditions:
1.  **Harsh Shadows:** When the downlights create distracting, hard-edged shadows on walls or furniture.
2.  **"Hot Spots":** When they create overly bright, blown-out circles on floors, bedding, or furniture, which the camera captures more harshly than the human eye.
3.  **Mixed Color Temperatures:** When the warm color of the downlights (e.g., 2700K) clashes with the cool color of daylight from the windows (e.g., 5500K+), creating an unappealing color cast that is difficult to correct. High-end photographers like Mike Kelley strongly advocate for turning them off to achieve a clean, single-source color palette.

A notable conflict exists between high-end architectural photography (which often turns practicals off) and faster-paced real estate photography, where some guides advise turning all lights on to make the space feel "lived-in and inviting."

### 7. Cove Lighting in Kelly's Classification and Pairing with Downlights

[PRACTICE] In Richard Kelly's classification of light, a cove is a quintessential example of the first layer: **"Ambient Luminescence."** Its purpose is to provide a general, diffuse, shadowless illumination that defines the volume of the space and makes people feel comfortable moving around. It is the "light to see by."

[PRACTICE] Published guidance on pairing cove with downlights focuses on the principle of layered lighting:
*   **When to Use Both:** Cove and downlights are used together when a complete, flexible lighting scheme is desired. The cove provides the soft, general *ambient* light, while downlights provide the *focal* glow (e.g., accenting art above the bed) or specific *task* lighting (e.g., aimed at a dresser or circulation path). This combination offers the most control and visual interest.
*   **When Cove Replaces Downlights:** A cove is used as the primary or sole ceiling source in bedrooms when the desired aesthetic is very soft, relaxing, and minimalist, similar to a high-end hotel room. In this case, task lighting is delegated entirely to bedside lamps, reading sconces, and perhaps floor lamps, eliminating any direct downward "pools" of light from the ceiling for a less visually cluttered and more serene effect.

### 8. Dusk vs. Daylight Photography and Window Luminance Ratio

[PRACTICE] Professional photography guides discuss shooting at both midday and blue hour (dusk) as aesthetic choices.
*   **Midday/Daylight:** Used to create a bright, airy, and fresh look. The challenge is controlling the high dynamic range between the bright exterior and the dimmer interior. Photographers use powerful strobes to raise the interior light level to better match the exterior.
*   **Blue Hour/Dusk:** Used to create a warm, cozy, and luxurious mood. At this time, the exterior and interior light levels are much closer, making it easier to achieve a natural-looking balance without extreme measures. The deep blue of the sky contrasts beautifully with the warm interior lights.

[PRACTICE] The targeted window-to-interior ratio is not a fixed number but is achieved through technique. The standard professional method, particularly advocated by photographer Scott Hargis, is to **set the camera's exposure for the window view first.**
1.  Set ISO and aperture (e.g., ISO 400, f/6.3).
2.  Adjust the shutter speed (e.g., up to 1/250s) until the view outside the window is perfectly exposed and not blown out.
3.  The interior will now be very dark. The photographer then adds off-camera flash, one light at a time, to bring the brightness of the interior up to match the correctly exposed window view.

[NO SOURCED VALUE FOUND] I could not find a specific, measured stop value or luminance ratio that practitioners target. [INFERENCE] The process is iterative ("chimping" the screen) until a visually pleasing balance is achieved where the window is clear and detailed, and the interior feels brightly and naturally lit.

## CONFLICTS AND UNCERTAINTY

*   **Practical Lights ON vs. OFF:** There is a direct conflict between advice for high-end architectural photography and general real estate photography.
    *   **Architectural (e.g., Mike Kelley):** Turn practical lights OFF during the day to avoid color contamination and create a clean, controlled image with a single light source color.
    *   **Real Estate (generic guides):** Turn all lights ON to make the space feel bright, lived-in, and inviting.
    *   **Resolution:** The choice depends on the goal. For architectural visualization aiming for a high-end, magazine look, the "lights off" approach is more authentic to professional practice. For a quick, bright real-estate shot, the "lights on" approach is common but less refined.

*   **Luminance Ratios:** While the IES provides clear [MEASURED] ratios for visual comfort (e.g., 10:1 max), the ratios in a photograph are a matter of [PRACTICE] and aesthetic choice. A photograph is not a simulation of the human eye's adaptation and will tolerate, or even require, higher contrast ratios to appear dynamic. The values for comfort should not be mistaken for targets for photographic representation.
