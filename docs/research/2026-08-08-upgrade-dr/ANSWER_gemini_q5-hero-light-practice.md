### 1. Photographers' Lighting Choices for Bedroom Hero Shots

[PRACTICE] Professional interior photographers often use a method of layering multiple exposures, turning different lights on and off in separate shots to have maximum control in post-production. The choice of which lights to use is dictated by the desired mood and the need to create a balanced, inviting image.

*   **Ceiling Downlights (often OFF or heavily dimmed):** Photographer Mike Kelley, known for his "flambient" technique, advises against using overhead ceiling lights (like can lights) as the primary light source because they create harsh, unflattering shadows and color casts that are difficult to correct. He states they "create nasty shadows and ugly color casts." [PRACTICE] (Mike Kelley, "The Ultimate Guide to Real Estate & Interior Photography," Fstoppers, 2017). Instead, he recommends turning them off and using flash to create a more natural and appealing light.
*   **Bedside Lamps & Sconces (ON):** These are almost always turned on. They act as "practical lights" that add a sense of warmth, life, and motivation to the scene. [PRACTICE] (Mike Kelley, "The Ultimate Guide to Real Estate & Interior Photography," Fstoppers, 2017). They create pools of light that add to the layering and depth of the image.
*   **Cove/Coffer Lighting (ON, often dimmed):** Cove lighting is used to add a soft, ambient glow and to highlight architectural details. It is typically kept on but may be dimmed to avoid it being the sole or overpowering source of light. The goal is to have it contribute to the overall ambient light without creating harshness. [PRACTICE] (Architectural Photography Almanac, "How to Light an Interior for Architectural Photography," no date).
*   **Decorative Pendants (ON):** Similar to bedside lamps, these are considered key practical lights that add character and a focal point. They are typically switched on to show their design and contribute to the layered lighting scheme. [PRACTICE] (Mike Kelley, "The Ultimate Guide to Real Estate & Interior Photography," Fstoppers, 2017).

The stated reason for these choices is control. By shooting separate exposures for ambient light (windows), practical lights (lamps), and flash, the photographer can blend them in post-production to create a perfectly balanced image that directs the viewer's eye, feels natural, and looks high-end.

### 2. Dominant Exposure Approach and Luminance Ratios

[PRACTICE] The dominant approach in high-end interior photography is a composite method, most notably the **"flambient" (flash + ambient) technique**. This involves blending a flash exposure with an ambient light exposure. While single exposures are used, and HDR is an option, the flambient method offers more precise control over the final look.

[PRACTICE] The goal for the luminance ratio between a visible lamp shade and the surrounding wall is to make the lamp look convincingly "on" without "blowing out" the highlights (losing all detail to pure white). Photographer Mike Kelley suggests a target of having the practical lights (like a lamp) appear about **one to two stops brighter** than the ambient light in the room. [PRACTICE] (Mike Kelley, "Where to Start With Lighting for Real Estate Photography," YouTube, 2020). This is achieved by shooting a separate exposure specifically for the lamps, often with the camera's exposure settings darkened, and then blending this into the main image using layer masks in Photoshop. He explicitly states that if the lamps are blown out in the ambient shot, you should "shoot a frame for the lamps" with a faster shutter speed to preserve the detail.

### 4. Dominant Lighting Layer in Marketing Photography

[PRACTICE] In professional real estate and marketing photography, the dominant visual layer is typically the **soft, bright, and even light from large windows (daylight)**, which is then supplemented and balanced with off-camera flash. The goal is to make the space look bright, airy, and inviting.

[PRACTICE] Professionals frequently **omit or turn off ceiling downlights** from the shot altogether. This is done under several conditions:
*   When the downlights create **unflattering "pools" of light and dark spots** on the floor or furniture.
*   When the downlights produce a strong **color cast** (e.g., overly warm or green) that is difficult to balance with the natural daylight and the photographer's flash (which is typically daylight-balanced).
*   When the downlights create **harsh shadows** from furniture or people, which detracts from the clean, open feel desired in marketing shots.

Photographer Mike Kelley explicitly advises turning them off: "The first thing I do when I walk into a room is turn all the lights off... especially the recessed can lights in the ceiling. Those are your worst enemy." [PRACTICE] (Mike Kelley, "The Ultimate Guide to Real Estate & Interior Photography," Fstoppers, 2017). The philosophy is that the photographer should be in complete control of the lighting, and existing ceiling lights are an unpredictable and often detrimental variable.

### 7. Cove Lighting in Kelly's Classification and Use with Downlights

[INFERENCE] In Richard Kelly's classification of light into "ambient luminescence," "focal glow," and "play of brilliants," cove lighting is a quintessential example of **ambient luminescence**. Its purpose is to provide a soft, indirect, and diffuse general illumination that fills shadows and defines the architecture of the space without drawing sharp attention to the source.

[PRACTICE] Published guidance suggests that the choice between using cove lighting alone or pairing it with downlights depends on the desired function and layering.
*   **When to use both:** Coves and downlights are often used together in a layered lighting scheme. The cove provides the soft ambient base layer, while downlights are added to provide "focal glow" or task lighting over specific areas, such as a reading chair or to highlight artwork. This combination creates a rich, flexible, and visually interesting environment. [PRACTICE] (Lutron, "Residential Lighting Design Guide," 2020).
*   **When a cove replaces downlights:** In bedrooms where a very soft, serene, and minimalist aesthetic is desired, cove lighting can be used as the primary source of general illumination, replacing downlights entirely. This approach eliminates the "pools" of light and potential glare from downlights, creating a more hotel-like, relaxing atmosphere. This is often supplemented with task lighting from bedside lamps or reading sconces. [PRACTICE] (WAC Lighting, "Indirect Lighting Design Guide," no date).

### 8. Dusk vs. Daylight Shooting and Window Exposure

[PRACTICE] Published interior photography guides discuss shooting at both midday and the "blue hour" (dusk), with the choice depending on the desired mood.
*   **Midday/Daylight:** Used for bright, airy, and cheerful shots. The goal is to show the space filled with natural light. The challenge is managing the high dynamic range between the bright exterior and the darker interior.
*   **Blue Hour/Dusk:** Used for more dramatic, moody, and luxurious shots. The deep blue sky provides a colorful backdrop, and the interior lights create a warm, inviting glow. This time is often preferred for high-end listings because the luminance of the sky is much closer to the luminance of the interior lighting, making it easier to balance the exposure.

[PRACTICE] To prevent the window view from being "blown out," photographers target a balanced luminance ratio. This is typically achieved by compositing multiple exposures.
*   **The "Flambient" Method:** A photographer will take one exposure for the interior (often with flash) and a separate, darker exposure for the window view. In his tutorials, Mike Kelley demonstrates adjusting the shutter speed to correctly expose for the view outside the window, which might be **2 to 4 stops darker** than the exposure for the interior. These are then blended in post-production. [PRACTICE] (Mike Kelley, "The Ultimate Guide to Real Estate & Interior Photography," Fstoppers, 2017). The goal is not necessarily a 1:1 ratio, but a believable view where the exterior is clearly visible and not a sheet of pure white, making the space feel connected to its surroundings.
