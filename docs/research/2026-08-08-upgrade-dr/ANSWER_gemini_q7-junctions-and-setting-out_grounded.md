### 1. WALL-TO-FLOOR

The primary options for a wall-to-floor junction in contemporary residential interiors are traditional timber/MDF skirting, flush skirting, and shadow gap/negative details. A "no detail at all" junction is technically challenging but conceptually pursued in minimalist design.

**Timber/MDF Skirting**
This is the most common and traditional approach.
*   **Height:**
    *   [PRACTICE] For modern interiors, heights often range from 70mm to 145mm.
    *   [PRACTICE] A common rule of thumb for balanced proportions is for the skirting height to be 7-8% of the ceiling height. For a standard 2.4m ceiling, this suggests a height of 168-192mm.
    *   [PRACTICE] UK suppliers note that the most popular selling height is 145mm, which is considered a versatile choice for most homes with standard 2.4m ceilings.
    *   [MEASURED] Off-the-shelf heights from UK suppliers like Champion Timber and Buttles range from 94mm up to 244mm.
*   **Projection (Thickness):**
    *   [PRACTICE] Smaller rooms in contemporary homes may suit slimmer profiles of 12-15mm to avoid an overwhelming appearance.
    *   [MEASURED] The most common thickness for MDF skirting is 18mm. Other available thicknesses are 14.5mm, 15mm, and 25mm.

**Flush Skirting**
This detail aims to have the skirting board finish perfectly flush with the plaster wall surface, eliminating the traditional projection.
*   **Dimensions:**
    *   [MEASURED] The FIP Porte flush-to-the-wall skirting system has a standard height of 70mm and a thickness of 12mm, which is recessed into the wall. The system uses an aluminum profile anchored to the wall to guide the plastering and hold the HDF skirting board.
    *   [MEASURED] AGS Systems provides flush skirting profiles for various insert materials (wood, aluminum, etc.) with flexible heights from approximately 45mm to 120mm. The system is designed to be installed before plastering.

**Negative Detail / Shadow Gap Skirting**
This detail creates a recess or "shadow line" between the wall and the floor or between the wall and a flush skirting element, making the wall appear to "float." This is a dominant detail in contemporary minimal interiors.
*   **Dimensions:**
    *   [MEASURED] **SA Solutions** offers gypsum-based profiles with shadow gap widths from 5mm to 12mm.
    *   [MEASURED] **EZ Concept** produces the AluBase Shadowline system, an aluminum profile that creates a 10mm x 10mm or 12mm x 12mm shadowline recess above a 90mm high flush skirt section. A US version specifies a 3/8" x 3/8" (approx. 9.5mm x 9.5mm) perimeter.
    *   [MEASURED] **Altro Architectural Building Systems**' Euro Skirt creates a 10mm shadowline. It is available in overall heights of 95mm and 135mm.
    *   [MEASURED] **Intrim's** Shadowline® system is a timber profile with an integrated shadow gap. The standard groove depth is 10mm, with a 5mm option also available.
    *   [MEASURED] **AGS Systems** offers an aluminum shadow gap profile (Model S22) with a shadow gap height of 22mm.
    *   [MEASURED] **Liwal International** supplies an aluminum profile system where the shadow gap height can be 10mm, 15mm, or 20mm.
    *   [MEASURED] An unbranded aluminum profile from **ASP Profiles** (P-1-40) has a recess height of 41.5mm. Another model (P-20) has a 20mm height.

**No Detail at All (Scribed/Plaster-to-Floor)**
*   [INFERENCE] A true "no detail" junction, where the finished plaster wall meets the finished floor in a single sharp line, is exceptionally difficult to build and maintain. It requires near-perfect levelness in the floor and plumbness in the wall, as any irregularity will be immediately visible as an inconsistent gap. It also offers no protection to the base of the plaster wall from impact, moisture from cleaning, or damage during floor installation.
*   [PRACTICE] The expansion and contraction gap required for most timber flooring (typically 10-15mm) must be concealed. A "no detail" approach would leave this gap visible. While it could be filled with a flexible sealant, achieving a clean, durable, and aesthetically pleasing finish is very challenging. The International Residential Code (IRC) notes that compressible floor coverings should not extend under walls fastened to the floor, indicating a separation is standard practice.
*   [INFERENCE] NO SOURCED VALUE FOUND for a buildable, standard system for a "no detail at all" junction in mainstream residential construction. This detail is more common in gallery or museum settings where floors might be poured and polished concrete, and the junction can be managed with a saw-cut control joint. For residential timber floors, it is generally considered unbuildable without a high risk of a poor-quality finish and future cracking.
