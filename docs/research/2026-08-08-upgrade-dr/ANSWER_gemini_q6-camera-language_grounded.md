### 1. Focal Lengths (35 mm equivalent) by Room Type and Size

[PRACTICE] **Imagtor (Real Estate Photography Focus):**
*   **Small Bathrooms, Powder Rooms, Compact Apartments:** 14–16mm. The source notes a high risk of distortion that requires correction.
*   **Living Rooms, Kitchens, Bedrooms:** 20–24mm is described as the "sweet spot" for a balance of wide coverage and natural perspective.
*   **Kitchens & Bathrooms (Specific):** 16–18mm is recommended to capture layout without distortion.
*   **Hallways & Transitional Spaces:** ~20mm. The source advises against using lenses wider than 16mm to avoid exaggerating narrowness.

[PRACTICE] **Archviz Blog (Rendering Focus):**
*   **Standard for Most Interior Rendering (e.g., Living Room):** 35mm is presented as the default, closest to natural human vision without distortion. A brief might specifically request a "35mm shot."
*   **Tight Spaces (e.g., small living room, narrow corridor, galley kitchen):** 24mm is the widest focal length used professionally to get the full layout in frame, with the trade-off of foreground perspective distortion.
*   **Detail/Hero Shots (e.g., furniture):** 50mm is used to isolate a single object.

[PRACTICE] **Ronen Bekerman (Architectural Visualization Artist):**
*   **General Archviz:** Advises avoiding focal lengths shorter than 30mm, often using between 35mm and 55mm to avoid the "criminally abused" wide-angle distortion common in the field.

[PRACTICE] **ArchDaily (Architectural Photography Guide):**
*   **General Interior Shots:** States that a focal length anywhere between 16-35mm is ideal.

[PRACTICE] **Jak Spedding (Interior Photographer):**
*   **General Interiors:** States he would not personally go wider than 16mm due to vignetting and distortion.
*   **Versatile Range:** Suggests 24mm to 35mm offers a good balance between showing the room and maintaining realistic proportions.
*   **Detail/Vignette:** A 50mm lens gives a field of view similar to the human eye and is useful for this purpose.

### 2. Camera Height Above Finished Floor

[PRACTICE] **General Guideline (Real Estate):**
*   A frequently cited range is 4 to 5 feet (approx. 1.22 m to 1.52 m) above the ground. Another source gives this as 4.5 to 5 feet (1.37 m to 1.52 m).
*   Imagtor gives a universal guideline of 1.2–1.4 meters, described as "close to eye level."

[PRACTICE] **Room-Specific Adjustments:**
*   **Kitchens and Bathrooms:** Shoot near countertop height, around 1.1–1.2 m. Another source suggests setting the camera 1.25 to 2 feet (0.38 m to 0.61 m) *above* the counter height. A common practitioner rule is to raise the camera just high enough to not see the underside of the upper cabinets.
*   **Living Rooms & Bedrooms:** Positioned at eye level (≈1.4 m). For living areas whose subject is furniture, a "seated height (waist level)" is recommended to make the viewer feel like they are in the room. For bedrooms, one guide suggests the camera should be 1.25 to 2 feet (0.38 m to 0.61 m) above the height of the bed. Another suggests the camera should be about 6 inches (0.15 m) higher than the bed.
*   **General Furniture:** One practitioner suggests a rule of thumb: "camera should be about 6" higher than the focal point of the shot. In a living room, 6" higher than the couch."

[PRACTICE] **Alternative Conventions:**
*   **"Shoot from the heart":** A guideline from architecture photographer Rob Moroto, corresponding to chest height.
*   **Midway between floor and ceiling:** Another mentioned practice, though it is noted this doesn't work well in rooms with very high ceilings as furniture heights remain constant.
*   **"Light switch level":** Mentioned by multiple practitioners as a good default height for most rooms.

### 3. Conventions for Vertical Shift / Horizon Placement

[PRACTICE] **Parallel Verticals:** The most consistent and emphasized convention across all sources is that the camera sensor must be perfectly level (zero tilt up or down) to ensure all vertical lines in the scene are parallel in the final image. Tilting the camera, even by a few degrees, causes verticals to converge, which is described as "one of the clearest markers of an amateur camera setup."
*   This is achieved by keeping the camera body perfectly level and using a tripod. Many cameras have a built-in digital level to assist with this.

[PRACTICE] **Horizon Placement:** When the camera is perfectly level, the horizon line naturally falls in the center of the frame. [INFERENCE] This establishes a neutral, non-distorted view.

[PRACTICE] **Vertical Shift (Rise/Fall):** A tilt-shift lens or digital equivalent (keystone correction) is used to adjust the composition vertically *without* tilting the camera body. This allows the photographer to include more of the ceiling or floor while keeping verticals parallel. The primary use is to control perspective and composition in-camera. ### 4. One-Point vs. Two-Point Perspective Selection Criteria

[PRACTICE] **One-Point Perspective (Straight-On View):**
*   **Criteria:** Chosen when the camera sensor can be placed perfectly parallel to a significant flat wall or feature. It is used to highlight symmetry, balance, and create a "regal" or "upscale" feeling.
*   **Use Cases:** Ideal for exteriors, hallways, feature walls, or long, rectangular spaces where the camera is placed on the centerline. It is described as difficult to master because it requires perfect alignment of the camera and the room's elements.

[PRACTICE] **Two-Point Perspective (Corner View):**
*   **Criteria:** Chosen when shooting into a corner of a room. It is used to show depth, dimensionality, and give a better sense of the space's layout by showing multiple walls.
*   **Use Cases:** The most common approach for showing the volume and flow of a room, particularly in real estate photography.

[INFERENCE] **Yaw Angle:** NO SOURCED VALUE FOUND that quantifies the yaw angle separating a one-point from a two-point perspective. The distinction is compositional rather than defined by a specific angle. A one-point perspective requires the camera's view direction to be perpendicular to the main subject plane (i.e., zero yaw relative to the plane's normal). Any significant deviation from this perpendicular alignment will introduce a second vanishing point, creating a two-point perspective.

### 5. Differences Between Listing/Marketing and Editorial/Portfolio Registers

[PRACTICE] **Core Goal:**
*   **Listing/Marketing:** To sell a property by showing as much of the space as possible, giving a clear idea of the floor plan and room sizes. The goal is information.
*   **Editorial/Portfolio:** To sell a "vibe" or tell a story about the design. The goal is to communicate craft, atmosphere, and design intention.

[PRACTICE] **Technique & Framing:**
*   **Listing/Marketing:**
    *   **Lens:** Tends to use wider-angle lenses to make rooms feel larger and capture more in a single frame.
    *   **Framing:** Shots are often taken from a corner to maximize the visible area and show multiple walls. The composition aims to show everything in the room.
    *   **Lighting:** Bright, even lighting is prioritized, often using flash or HDR to make spaces look clean and legible, sometimes at the expense of natural mood.
*   **Editorial/Portfolio:**
    *   **Lens:** May use longer focal lengths (e.g., 35mm, 50mm) to create vignettes and focus on details. [INFERENCE]
    *   **Framing:** More selective and compositional. What is left *out* of the frame is as important as what is included. Asymmetric framing and vertical orientations are more common.
    *   **Lighting:** More emphasis on mood, shadow, and natural light to create atmosphere. The editing process is more extensive and detailed.

### 6. Orientation and Aspect Ratio by Medium

[PRACTICE] **Native Camera Ratios:** Most full-frame and APS-C cameras have a native sensor aspect ratio of 3:2. Micro Four Thirds cameras are typically 4:3.

[PRACTICE] **MLS Listings:**
*   The most commonly recommended aspect ratios are 4:3 and 3:2.
*   Some sources note that 4:3 is a legacy ratio from older monitors but remains common.
*   A wider 16:9 ratio can be used for panoramic or wide exterior shots.
*   A vertical 3:4 ratio can be used for tall spaces like foyers.

[PRACTICE] **Social Media:**
*   **Instagram:** 1:1 (square) for posts and 4:5 for vertical posts are common. Stories use a vertical 9:16 ratio.
*   **Facebook:** Recommends an image size corresponding to a 1.91:1 aspect ratio.

[PRACTICE] **Print & Architectural Use:**
*   A 4:5 ratio has been commonly used for photographing architecture.
*   The 1:1 (square) ratio is sometimes used to bring formality and symmetry, common in fine art photography.

### 7. Quantitative Signatures of Amateur vs. Professional Images

[PRACTICE] **Convergence of Verticals:** This is the most frequently cited differentiator. Professional images maintain parallel vertical lines. Amateur images often show verticals converging (leaning inwards or outwards) due to the camera being tilted up or down. NO SOURCED VALUE FOUND for a specific tolerance in degrees.

[PRACTICE] **Lens Distortion:** Professionals use high-quality rectilinear lenses and/or software correction to minimize barrel distortion (where straight lines bow outwards) and perspective distortion (where objects at the edge of a wide shot appear stretched). Amateur shots with very wide, uncorrected lenses often exhibit noticeable distortion.

[PRACTICE] **Framing of Floor and Ceiling:** Professionals are deliberate about the balance of floor and ceiling.
*   They avoid showing the underside of kitchen cabinets, which happens when the camera is too low.
*   They adjust camera height to feature more of an interesting floor or a vaulted ceiling, and less of a plain, uninteresting ceiling. Amateurs may shoot from a default eye-level, creating an awkward "looking down" perspective that shows too much floor or cuts off furniture.

[PRACTICE] **Subject Placement & Composition:**
*   Professionals use one-point and two-point perspectives deliberately to create balance or depth.
*   Amateur photos may lack a clear compositional focal point or have awkward framing, such as shooting the back of a couch which "swallows up your whole shot."

## CONFLICTS AND UNCERTAINTY

*   **Camera Height:** There is no single standard for camera height. Sources provide a wide range of conflicting [PRACTICE] conventions:

    *   Anatomical references: "Chest height", "waist height", "mid-torso", "eye level" (which is often discouraged as too high).
    *   Environmental references: "Light switch level", "midway between floor and ceiling", "just high enough to not see under cabinets", or a height relative to the main furniture ("6 inches above the couch").
    *   This variety suggests that camera height is highly context-dependent and subject to practitioner preference, making a single studio constant unreliable.

*   **Focal Length:** There is a clear conflict based on the photography register.
    *   **Real Estate / Listing:** Practitioners embrace wider lenses (14-18mm) to show space, accepting the need for distortion correction.
    *   **Archviz / Editorial:** Practitioners often advocate for tighter, more "natural" focal lengths (24mm, 35mm, or even 50mm) and explicitly warn against the distortion of ultra-wide lenses.
    *   A builder should not assume a single "correct" focal length; it depends entirely on whether the goal is to document space (wider) or convey an experience (tighter).
