### 1. WALL-TO-FLOOR

### 2. SHADOW GAP VISIBILITY

A shadow gap detail is visible in a photograph taken from 2–4 meters away.

*   **Tonal Appearance:** It reads as a sharp, dark, and consistent line of shadow. The darkness is deep and uniform because the light from typical interior sources (which are usually above) cannot easily illuminate the top surface of the recess [INFERENCE]. The shadow is crisper and darker than a simple dark line drawn on a wall because it has depth.
*   **Visual Cues:** What makes it read as a gap rather than a drawn line are subtle lighting effects [INFERENCE]:
    1.  **Sharpness of the Shadow Edge:** The upper edge of the gap, formed by the plasterbead, is very sharp, creating a crisp, defined start to the shadow.
    2.  **Occlusion:** The detail physically recedes. From most viewing angles, the upper surface of the recess is self-shadowed or occluded, creating a depth that a simple line lacks.
    3.  **No Specular Reflection:** A painted line might have a slight sheen or specular reflection that a true shadow gap will not have in its recess [INFERENCE].
*   **Width in Pixels:** This is highly dependent on the camera's sensor resolution, lens focal length, distance, and the final image resolution. However, for a typical 10mm wide gap photographed from 3 meters with a standard lens (e.g., 35mm on a full-frame sensor) for a final 4K (3840px wide) image, the gap might occupy approximately 5-10 pixels in width [INFERENCE]. This is a rough estimation, but it highlights that it is a subtle but distinct detail.

### 3. DOOR AND OPENING

*   **Architrave:** The traditional timber or MDF moulding that frames the opening.
    *   **Dimensions:** Widths typically range from 45mm to 95mm, with a thickness of 18mm or 25mm [PRACTICE]. A common contemporary profile is a simple flat "square dressed" board of around 68mm x 18mm [PRACTICE].

*   **Shadow-Gap Frameless:** The door frame is hidden within the wall, and a small gap or "reveal" is left between the wall finish and the door jamb, creating a shadow line.
    *   **Dimensions:** The shadow gap is typically between 5mm and 12mm wide [PRACTICE].
    *   **Manufacturer Systems:**
        *   **EzyJamb Classic Split (SRC):** This system creates a nominal 10mm shadowline reveal around the door jamb [MEASURED] (Studco Building Systems, "EzyJamb SRC," 2022). It is designed for standard 92mm studs with 13mm plasterboard on each side.
        *   **Argenta Invisidoor DL:** A frameless door system where the gap between the door panel and the wall finish can be adjusted, but is typically set to a few millimeters [PRACTICE].

*   **Plaster Return (or Square Set):** The plasterboard wraps directly around into the door opening, with a corner bead to create a sharp, clean edge. There is no separate architrave.
    *   **Dimensions:** The "dimension" is the thickness of the plasterboard and corner bead, typically 10mm or 13mm [PRACTICE].

*   **Threshold Detail (Frame to Timber Floor):**
    *   The standard practice is for the timber flooring to be installed *after* the door frame/jambs. The flooring is cut to fit around the jamb, leaving a small expansion gap (typically 3-5mm) which is then concealed by the architrave [PRACTICE].
    *   For frameless/architrave-free details, the door jamb must be installed with precision relative to the finished floor height. The jamb is often undercut slightly, allowing the timber flooring to slide underneath it by a few millimeters. This creates a very clean, tight junction with no visible gap [PRACTICE]. This requires a high level of coordination and craftsmanship.

### 4. JOINERY SETTING-OUT

*   **Panel Widths:**
    *   The **AWMAC Architectural Woodwork Standards (AWS), Section 10 - Casework** states that "reveals between adjacent cabinet doors and drawer fronts shall be uniform in width" [MEASURED] (AWMAC, "Architectural Woodwork Standards," 2014). It does not explicitly mandate that all door/bay widths in a run must be equal, but it is a strong convention in high-quality work for aesthetic reasons [PRACTICE]. The primary requirement is for uniformity of the gaps. Unequal door widths are often used to accommodate specific internal fittings or site conditions, but this is typically a deliberate design choice [PRACTICE]. Therefore, equal bays are a **convention**, not a strict requirement, but uniform reveals *are* a requirement.

*   **Alignment:**
    *   The AWMAC standards emphasize visual harmony. Section 10 requires that "the top and bottom edges of all abutting cabinet doors and drawer fronts shall be in alignment" [MEASURED] (AWMAC, "Architectural Woodwork Standards," 2014). While not explicitly stating alignment with external elements like bulkheads, it is a core principle of good "setting out" in practice to align joinery lines with major architectural datums where possible [PRACTICE].

*   **Reveal/Gap Width:**
    *   The AWMAC standards specify tolerances for reveals. For Premium Grade casework, the reveal between doors/drawers shall be "uniform at 2 mm (± 0.8 mm)" [MEASURED] (AWMAC, "Architectural Woodwork Standards," 2014). For Custom Grade, the tolerance is slightly looser. Therefore, a 2mm gap is a common specified dimension.

### 7. TOLERANCE

*   **Construction Tolerances:**
    *   **Plumb and Level:** The American standard **ASTM C840** ("Standard Specification for Application and Finishing of Gypsum Board") sets tolerances for drywall installation. For levels of finish intended for non-decorated areas, a tolerance might be wider, but for high-quality painted surfaces, the expectation is much tighter. A common industry benchmark for high-quality work is a deviation of no more than 3mm over a 2.4m (8ft) length for plumb (vertical) and level (horizontal) [PRACTICE]. The British Standard **BS 8000-8:1994** ("Workmanship on building sites. Code of practice for plasterboard partitions and dry linings") specifies a tolerance for verticality of a surface as not deviating by more than 8mm over a 3m height [MEASURED].
    *   **Gap Variation:** For joinery, as noted above, AWMAC Premium Grade requires a 2mm reveal with a tolerance of ±0.8mm, meaning the gap can vary from 1.2mm to 2.8mm [MEASURED] (AWMAC, "Architectural Woodwork Standards," 2014).

*   **Tolerance in Renders:**
    *   There is no formal standard, but there is extensive practitioner opinion. The consensus is that perfectly aligned, mathematically exact models look artificial and "CG".
    *   **Guidance:** Practitioners recommend deliberately introducing minor imperfections to enhance realism. This includes:
        *   Slight variations in the straightness of long lines (e.g., wall/floor junctions).
        *   Minute, non-uniform gaps and reveals.
        *   Slight rounding or chamfering of sharp edges (a "bevel" modifier in software) to mimic how light catches real-world edges.
        *   Using textures that are not perfectly tiled and show subtle wear or variation.
    *   This is a widely discussed topic in forums like CGArchitect and in tutorials from artists like those at Ronen Bekerman's blog or State of Art Academy [PRACTICE]. The goal is to break the "computer perfection" that gives away a render.

### 8. OMITTED DETAILS AND REALISM
