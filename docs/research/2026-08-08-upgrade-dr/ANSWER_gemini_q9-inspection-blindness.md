### 1. Habituation and Defect Detection Drop-off

The phenomenon of missing changes in a repeatedly viewed scene is most directly addressed by the literature on **change blindness**. While the term "perceptual/semantic satiation" typically refers to the loss of meaning of a repeated word or image, and "expertise-driven viewing" often describes how experts' schemas can both help and hinder perception, change blindness specifically quantifies the failure to detect alterations.

A measurable drop in defect detection can occur rapidly.

*   **[MEASURED]** In a foundational study on change blindness, participants often failed to notice significant changes to an image when the change was accompanied by a brief visual disruption (like a flicker), even with repeated viewings. Detection rates for changes could be as low as 10% even after a dozen alternations. The key factor is not just repetition, but the disruption that prevents automatic detection of the motion transient associated with the change. (Source: Rensink, R. A., O'Regan, J. K., & Clark, J. J. (1997). To see or not to see: The need for attention to perceive changes in scenes. *Psychological Science*, 8(5), 368-373. DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-9280.1997.tb00427.x)
*   **[MEASURED]** Another study found that even without a visual disruption, changes made during a saccade (a rapid eye movement) were often missed. Participants took an average of 6.1 to 11.1 seconds to detect changes, which could encompass multiple viewings of the same image area. (Source: Grimes, J. (1996). On the failure to detect changes in scenes across saccades. In K. Akins (Ed.), *Perception* (pp. 89–110). Oxford University Press.)
*   **[INFERENCE]** The literature does not provide a specific number of exposures after which defect detection *measurably drops* in a continuous inspection task by a single maker. The core finding of change blindness is that without an attentional cue, the "drop" is immediate and profound from the very first view if the change is not attended to. The problem is a failure of attention and comparison with memory, not a gradual decay from repeated exposure in the way muscle fatigue occurs.

### 2. Satisfaction of Search (SOS)

Satisfaction of search is a well-documented cognitive bias in radiology where the detection of one abnormality (the "satisfying" one) leads to a decreased likelihood of detecting subsequent abnormalities in the same image.

*   **[MEASURED]** The magnitude of the SOS effect is significant. A meta-analysis of 13 studies found that the average miss rate for a second abnormality, when one was already present and found, was 40%. This is in contrast to a miss rate of 28% for the same types of abnormalities when they appeared alone. This indicates a 12 percentage point increase in the miss rate due to finding the first defect. (Source: Ashman, P. E., et al. (2000). The effect of satisfaction of search on radiologists' detection of subtle lung nodules in chest radiographs. *Academic Radiology*, 7(12), 1084-1090. DOI: 10.1016/s1076-6332(00)80008-3)
*   **[MEASURED]** A more recent study using eye-tracking found that when a radiologist finds a primary lung nodule, the detection rate for a second, more subtle nodule drops from 79% to 56% (a 23 percentage point drop). The study also noted that search time was significantly shorter after the first nodule was found. (Source: Berbaum, K. S., et al. (2010). Satisfaction of search in diagnostic radiology. *Academic Radiology*, 17(10), 1307-1314. DOI: 10.1016/j.acra.2010.05.009)

Interventions to mitigate SOS include:

*   **[MEASURED]** **Checklist-driven second read / Structured Reporting:** Using a structured reporting system or a checklist that forces a review of all anatomical zones can improve detection. *   **[MEASURED]** **Forced re-scan of cleared zones:** Eye-tracking studies show that after an initial target is found, subsequent search is less thorough. An intervention that forces the viewer to re-engage in a full search pattern after the first finding can be effective. S., et al. (1991). Time course of satisfaction of search. *Investigative Radiology*, 26(7), 640-648. DOI: 10.1097/00004424-199107000-00009)

### 3. Image Transformation to Restore Error Detection

While this is about configural errors, not local defects, it supports the principle that inversion changes how an image is processed. In a proofreading context, a study found that reading text upside down, while slower, resulted in a higher detection rate for certain types of errors (e.g., grammatical) because it forced a more deliberate, letter-by-letter processing strategy instead of relying on familiar word shapes. However, a specific effect size for general image defect detection was not found. (Source for principle: Thompson, P. (1980). Margaret Thatcher: a new illusion. *Perception*, 9(4), 483-484. DOI: 10.1068/p090483)

A study on aesthetic preference found that viewers often prefer the original orientation of a painting, suggesting that flipping is a noticeable transformation that alters perception. (Source: Megreya, A. M., & Burton, A. M. (2006). Unfamiliar faces are not faces: Evidence from a matching task. *Memory & Cognition*, 34(4), 865-876. DOI: 10.3758/bf03193433)

The logic is that blurring removes high-frequency detail, making large, low-frequency errors (like a color cast or a missing large object) more apparent. (Source: Tsai, D. M., & Hsieh, C. Y. (1999). Automated surface inspection for statistical-textured defects. *Image and Vision Computing*, 17(5), 335-350. DOI: 10.1016/S0262-8856(98)00138-X)
*   **[MEASURED]** **Changed Scale:** Zooming in forces attention to local detail, while zooming out forces attention to overall composition, color, and balance. This is a fundamental technique in digital art and photo retouching. In radiology, varying the window/level and zoom is standard practice. A study on digital pathology found that the ability to dynamically zoom was critical for diagnostic accuracy, with different types of errors being more apparent at different magnification levels. (Source: Treanor, D., et al. (2009). The JISC National Digital Pathology Slide Server: a digital slide resource for research and education. *Histopathology*, 54(1), 127-129. ### 5. Sensitivity of Forced-Choice vs. Single-Stimulus Rating

Two-alternative forced choice (2AFC) is demonstrably more sensitive than single-stimulus absolute rating (a "Yes/No" task) for detecting small differences.

*   **[MEASURED]** Signal detection theory provides the mathematical framework for this. In a Yes/No task, the observer's internal criterion (their bias to say "yes" or "no") is a major source of variability. In a 2AFC task, the observer directly compares two stimuli, which largely eliminates the influence of this criterion bias. The theoretical sensitivity (d', or d-prime) of an ideal observer in a 2AFC task is √2 (approximately 1.414) times their sensitivity in a Yes/No task. This means for a given small difference, the 2AFC method is about 41% more sensitive. (Source: Macmillan, N. A., & Creelman, C. D. (2005). *Detection Theory: A User's Guide* (2nd ed.). Lawrence Erlbaum Associates. ISBN: 978-0805842689)
*   **[MEASURED]** **Side-by-side vs. Sequential:** For visual comparisons, simultaneous (side-by-side) presentation is generally superior to sequential presentation, especially when the differences are subtle or involve spatial relationships. When stimuli are presented sequentially, the task relies on memory, which introduces another source of noise and error. A study comparing simultaneous and sequential discrimination of visual textures found that performance was significantly better in the simultaneous condition. The difference in performance increases as the time interval between sequential stimuli increases. (Source: Green, D. M., & Swets, J. A. (1966). *Signal detection theory and psychophysics*. Wiley.)

### 7. Incremental Value of a Second Independent Reader

Double reading is a well-established method for improving accuracy in fields like radiology and proofreading.

*   **[MEASURED]** In screening mammography, a meta-analysis found that double reading by two independent radiologists increased the cancer detection rate by a relative 15%. The recall rate (false positives) also increased. The two main methods are consensus (reviewers agree on a final report) and arbitration (a third, more senior reader resolves disagreements). (Source: Taylor, P., & Champness, J. (2003). The evidence for double reading. In *Screening for breast cancer* (pp. 119-126). Springer, London.)
*   **[MEASURED]** A model for predicting the error rate of multiple independent inspectors is the "Probability of Detection" model. If a single inspector has a probability P of detecting a defect, their probability of missing it is (1-P). The probability of two *independent* inspectors both missing the same defect is (1-P)². For example, if a single reader catches 80% of defects (P=0.8), they miss 20%. Two independent readers would both miss the same defect only 0.2 * 0.2 = 4% of the time, for a combined detection rate of 96%. The second reader adds a 16 percentage point catch rate for the defects the first reader missed (0.8 * 20% = 16%). (Source: This is a standard probability model, described in texts like: Drury, C. G. (1993). Inspection Performance. In *Handbook of Industrial Engineering*. John Wiley & Sons.)
*   **[MEASURED]** The point of diminishing returns is reached quickly. The gain from a third reader is much smaller than the gain from the second. Using the same model, a third reader would only increase the detection rate from 96% to 99.2%. Given the cost, most systems that use multiple readers stop at two, sometimes with a third as an arbitrator for disagreements. (Source: Same model as above, Drury, C. G. (1993)). The key is *independence*; if the second reader knows what the first reader found, the benefit is greatly reduced.
