## 1. Retail business typology (6 types)

**Definition** (`files/485396_unknown.png`, WEEK 1): ธุรกิจค้าปลีก (retail business) = *กิจการที่
เกี่ยวข้องกับการซื้อขายสินค้า หรือบริการโดยตรงแก่ผู้บริโภคเพื่อตอบสนองความต้องการของผู้บริโภคคนสุดท้าย* — trade in goods or
services **direct to the final consumer** (ผู้บริโภคคนสุดท้าย), for that consumer's own use.

The deck enumerates six types. Types 1–4 are on `files/485396_unknown.png`; types 5–6 continue on
`files/720166_unknown.png`. Sub-numbering below is the deck's own (1.1, 1.2, …); the exemplar
brands are the deck's, not ours.

| # | Type | Attributes as listed by the deck | Exemplars named |
|---|------|----------------------------------|-----------------|
| 1 | **หาบเร่** (peddler / mobile vendor) | 1.1 brings the goods or service to the consumer's door (deck's example: a rice-and-curry cart working a village) · 1.2 sells at the general market price | — |
| 2 | **ร้านค้าเฉพาะอย่าง** — Specialty store | 2.1 sells one specific category and one specific brand (deck's example: goods of the body-hygiene category, สุขอนามัยของร่างกาย) · 2.2 carries a varied and complete assortment *within* that category, with category expertise | ห้างวัตสัน (Watsons) |
| 3 | **ร้านสะดวกซื้อ** — Convenience store | 3.1 goods necessary to daily life · 3.2 fast food and drinks · 3.3 sited for convenience · 3.4 **24-hour service** | 7-Eleven, FamilyMart |
| 4 | **ร้านสรรพาหาร** — Supermarket | 4.1 fresh food and consumables (เครื่องบริโภค) · 4.2 daily consumer goods · 4.3 food products that are fresh and varied · 4.4 sited inside a shopping centre, and also as a standalone store | Foodland Supermarket |
| 5 | **ซูเปอร์เซ็นเตอร์** — Supercenter / hypermarket | 5.1 daily-necessity consumer goods, **One stop shopping** in a single location · 5.2 wide variety, aimed at a clearly-defined customer group · 5.3 sets a price policy that appeals to / attracts customers (ดึงดูดใจ) · 5.4 **Self-Service** · 5.5 the customer service area is arranged on a **single floor** (พื้นที่บริการลูกค้าจัดเป็นชั้นเดียว) | Big C, Tesco Lotus, Carrefour |
| 6 | **ห้างสรรพสินค้า** — Department store | 6.1 general merchandise · 6.2 wide variety of kinds to choose from · 6.3 emphasises the product and **fashion-style display** (การจัดโชว์แบบแฟชั่น) · 6.4 expensive goods · 6.5 target customers **mid- to high-level** · 6.6 **Full-Service** · 6.7 selling area divided **into floors**, with goods grouped **into departments** (แผนก) | Central, The Mall, Terminal |

**Programming lever (ours, flagged as ours):** rows 5 and 6 are the deck's own attribute lists,
and read against each other they oppose on three axes that a layout must resolve — service model
(**Self-Service** 5.4 vs **Full-Service** 6.6), vertical organisation (**single floor** 5.5 vs
**floors + departments** 6.7), and price/target (a customer-attracting price policy 5.3 vs
expensive goods + mid-to-high target 6.4/6.5). The deck does not draw this contrast in prose; the
rows are the source, the contrast is our arithmetic on them.

## 2. Store-space program checklist

`files/622505_unknown.png` (WEEK 1) splits the shop into a display side and a support side.

- **พื้นที่สำหรับการจัดแสดงภายในร้าน** (area for in-store display) — the slide's list, verbatim and in order:
  **Shelf · Product · Advertise · Item · Prop. · Customer Flow**
- **ORGANIZING STORE SPACE : พื้นที่เก็บของ พื้นที่พนักงาน** (storage area, staff area) — called out on the
  slide as: **Counter Cashier · Storage**

So the deck's retail space program = 6 display elements + Counter Cashier + Storage + staff area.
Note that **Customer Flow is listed as a program element**, on the same footing as a shelf or a
prop. (The reading that this makes flow a *designed element* rather than a by-product of the plan
is **ours**; the deck supplies only the list — `files/622505_unknown.png` is a bare list of words
and carries no prose at all.) Flow as a drawn layer is what §4 and §5 then show.

## 3. Design psychology: behaviour, aesthetics, management

`files/230612_unknown.png` (WEEK 1), heading แนวคิดจิตวิทยาในการออกแบบร้านค้า อาคารพาณิชย์ (psychological
concepts in the design of shops and commercial buildings). **The printed body text and the
lecturer's annotation on the same slide disagree, and both are recorded here:**

- The **printed** text says the concepts relevant to the designer divide into **2 sides**:
  1. ด้านพฤติกรรม (**Behavior**) 2. ด้านสุนทรียภาพ (**Aesthetics**).
- The **hand-drawn** Venn on the same slide shows **3** overlapping circles — พฤติกรรม (behaviour) ·
  สุนทรียภาพ (aesthetics) · **บริหารจัดการ (management)** — whose common intersection is labelled
  **การออกแบบร้านค้าปลีก** (retail store design). The behaviour circle is annotated "USER Behavior".

A further hand-drawn sequence on that slide reads **เข้า → ดึงดูด → จ่าย → ออก** (enter → attract → pay →
exit). It is handwritten and partly illegible; treat it as an annotation, not as deck doctrine.

**Behaviour side** (`files/940244_unknown.png`, WEEK 1) — the deck's stated premise is that to
design a successful retail store the designer must understand the consumer's thinking and
decision process, which it calls a phenomenon with many overlapping dimensions. Its flow chart is
ผู้บริโภค (consumer) → กระบวนการซื้อสินค้า (the purchase process) → ปัจจัยที่มีอิทธิพล (influencing factors),
branching into three factor groups:

| Factor group | Factors listed |
|---|---|
| **ปัจจัยส่วนบุคคล** (personal) | ประชากร (demographic) · เศรษฐกิจ (economic) |
| **ปัจจัยด้านสังคม** (social) | ครอบครัว (family) · กลุ่ม (group) · ชนชั้น (class) · วัฒนธรรม (culture) |
| **ปัจจัยด้านจิตวิทยา** (psychological) | การรับรู้ (perception) · การจูงใจ (motivation) · การเรียนรู้ (learning) · ทัศนคติ (attitude) · บุคลิกภาพ (personality) |

## 4. Fixture arrangement typologies (ARRANGEMENT and CUSTOMER FLOW)

WEEK 3 names **six** arrangement types, one per slide, under the running head *ARRANGEMENT and
CUSTOMER FLOW*. **The deck gives each type a NAME and a plan diagram and no prose definition
whatsoever.** The "plan character" column below is therefore a *description of the drawing*, not
the deck's words, and is marked as such.

| Type (deck's name) | Slide | Plan character (our description of the drawing) | Annotation on the slide |
|---|---|---|---|
| **STRAIGHT** | `files/862633_unknown.png` | orthogonal fixture runs, parallel to the walls and to each other | ระเบียบ (orderly) |
| **ANGULAR** | `files/843530_unknown.png` | a continuous free-form / rounded fixture perimeter with circular islands; entry arrow from one side | — |
| **DIAGONAL** | `files/469830_unknown.png` | fixtures set on the diagonal; the **CASH** counter is drawn as a diamond in the centre of the plan; a dashed line traces the entry path in from one corner | ทะแยง (diagonal) |
| **PATHWAY** | `files/122354_unknown.png` (clean) and `files/536936_unknown.png` (annotated) | one highlighted (green) path leads from the entry deep into the plan; on the annotated copy, red arrows branch off that path and thread between the fixtures | — |
| **VARIETY** | `files/439899_unknown.png` | mixed: perimeter wall runs (marked in green) combined with free-standing island fixtures | — |
| **GEOMETRY** | `files/051082_unknown.png` | geometric island units (diamonds, triangles) standing free of the walls; the wall runs are marked green | **Stand Alone** |

The PATHWAY pair is the clearest statement of the deck's method: the *same* plan is shown clean
and then over-drawn with the customer-flow arrows, i.e. the flow is drawn **onto** an arrangement,
as a second layer.

## 5. Merchandise zoning against the customer-flow circuit

WEEK 4, running head *MERCHANDISE* (`files/339961_unknown.png` and, with a further annotation
layer, `files/439060_unknown.png`). Fixture blocks on the plan are colour-coded by purchase
motive. The legend is written on the slide, verbatim:

> **Demand** (open circle marker) · **convenience** (grey dot) · **Luxury / Impulse** (pink dot)

(That is the slide's own order and line-breaking: pink dot + "Luxury /" on the upper line, pink dot
+ "Impulse" beneath.)

Other elements labelled on the same sketch plan:

- **Directory** — written at the **top of the sheet**, above the plan. The deck marks **no entrance**
  anywhere on the slide, so the drawing does not tell us that the directory sits at an entry.
- **Storage** — written at the **rear/side** of the plan, outside the shopping floor.
- **Customer flow** — a magenta arrowed circuit drawn through the plan, past the coded blocks. Its
  two open ends are both on the **left edge** (one arrow running in, one running out).
- **Merchandise categories** — on `files/439060_unknown.png` only, three goods categories are
  circled across the top of the sheet and **leader-lined down onto specific fixture zones** of the
  plan: **รองเท้า** (shoes) · **กระเป๋า** (bags) · **หมวก** (hats). This is the deck's one worked example of
  assigning actual goods categories to zones — the concrete instance of the method stated below.

**What the sketch shows** (our reading of the drawing): the Demand and convenience blocks make up
the perimeter and mid-floor runs that the arrowed circuit is pulled around, and the Luxury/Impulse
(pink) blocks sit *attached to* those runs, on the edge the circuit already passes. **The deck
writes no rule-of-thumb to this effect** — there is no sentence on the slide explaining *why* a
class sits where it sits. Do not promote the placement into a stated rule; what is authoritative
here is the **method**: classify every fixture block by purchase motive, then draw the customer
circuit past the classified blocks, with storage off the shopping floor.

The same three classes are applied over a **STRAIGHT** arrangement plan on
`files/870471_unknown.png` (WEEK 3): "Demand" is written against the deep wall runs in yellow,
"conv." and "Lux." label blocks nearer the entry side, "WIND" marks the shopfront glazing, and red
arrows trace the flow in from the entry. This is the one slide where the WEEK 3 arrangement layer
and the WEEK 4 merchandise layer are drawn on top of each other.

## 6. Window display

Running head *WINDOW DISPLAY*.

**Start from the target** (`files/203749_unknown.png`, marked "Fundamental"): the slide's two
underlined headings are **TARGET GROUP** and **TARGET PERSONALITY**. Its worked example, written in
Thai across the top of a photographed fashion vitrine, is: ผู้หญิง (women) · แฟชั่น (fashion) · มั่นใจ
(confident) — plus one further token that is not legible in the staged image. The vitrine itself
is annotated **Clean**.

**PRESTIGE DISPLAY** (`files/314248_unknown.png`) — a named display type. Its handwritten chain
reads ผู้ดู / เดิน → เอะใจ / หยุดดู → เดินเข้า : the passer-by walks past, is caught (เอะใจ = "made to wonder")
and stops to look, and then walks in. **ศักดิ์ศรี** (prestige, dignity) is circled as the quality the
designer composes for. This is handwritten annotation, hedged accordingly.

**Composition vocabulary**, annotated by the lecturer on real vitrines
(`files/547924_unknown.png`, a Cartier window; `files/203749_unknown.png`, a Le Château window):

| Term | As written on the slide |
|---|---|
| **จุดเด่น / เอกภาพ** | focal point / unity — drawn onto the single hero object (the leaping panther) |
| **Asymmetrical Balance** | circled top-right, hand-lettered **"Assemitical Balance"** (the lecturer's own misspelling); illustrated beside it with a seesaw sketch |
| **Group** | "Group ⇒ หมู่" — the supporting objects are outlined together as one mass |
| **Clean** | the quality called out on the fashion vitrine |

These are directly reusable as concept-brief and render-composition language for a storefront
view. They are compositional vocabulary, not measurable criteria — nothing here scores an image.

## 7. Corporate identity: the three Findings, and the personality palette

WEEK 5, running head *CORPORATE IDENTITY*, under the question **"What's design?"**
(`files/841724_unknown.png`). The slide's three F-headings, in order:

1. **Finding Target Group**
2. **Finding Target Group Personality**
3. **Finding Design Personality**

i.e. the design personality is derived from the target group's personality, which is derived from
the target group — the same persona→parameter chain the residential side uses, applied to a shop.
The handwritten line beneath reads, in substance, that the aim is to design the shop to **match
the character of the goods** it sells.

adjective list with its own Thai glosses, verbatim:

## 8. Honest gaps (what this file deliberately does not carry)

- **No dimensions, at all.** No aisle width, shelf height, counter height, vitrine depth or
  clearance appears anywhere in the 22 slides. The one handwritten number in the deck sits in the
  right margin of the WEEK 4 merchandise plan (`files/439060_unknown.png`), beside the
  Luxury/Impulse legend, and reads **รองเท้า** (shoes) / **3,000 +** / **limited.** — i.e. it is a
  **merchandise-class worked example** for the shoes category, **not a dimension**, and it is not
  promoted as one. No unit is written and **no ฿ symbol appears**, so it is not promoted as a price
  either.
- **No statutory content.** Nothing in the deck is a code value. Thai statutory requirements for
  commercial buildings / shophouses are `knowledge/codes-th/`'s business, not this file's, and
  `codes-th/` outranks everything here.
- **No prose definitions for the six arrangement types** (§4) — the deck supplies names and
  drawings only. Anyone who needs a definition must get it from a real retail-planning source, not
  from an inference off these plans.
- **Two staged slides carry no design payload and were dropped:**
  `files/379776_unknown.png` and `files/346812_unknown.png` are course-administration notes
  (thesis clip-VDO submission, shared folders, proposal paperwork, a site-location/COVID-19
  comment). Nothing in them concerns retail design.
