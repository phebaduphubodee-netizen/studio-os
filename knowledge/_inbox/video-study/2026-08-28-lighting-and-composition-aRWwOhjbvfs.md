## 1. THE SENTENCE THIS PROJECT NEEDED

`[03:16]` — *"firstly, **please no more grids of downlights on the ceiling.** As we've just
seen, the grid layout produces a very flat and lifeless effect — **everything is lit with
the same intensity.** To provide visually stimulating lighting effects we need a carefully
considered balance of light and shadow. It's this **variation of intensity** that adds
dynamism to any space."*

**Our room has twelve recessed downlights in a grid, and every critic has called the frame
flat.** A luxury lighting design director names that exact arrangement as the cause, not a
symptom.

He proves it before he says it, on one bas-relief lit four ways `[00:56]`-`[02:19]`:

| Lighting | His verdict |
|---|---|
| natural light, direct or reflected | *"ambient and diffused… quite a **flat** lighting effect, also relatively **uncontrollable** — you have to take what you're given"* |
| **a classic grid of downlights** | *"is there any improvement here? I feel it's still **quite disappointing**, and the piece feels **lifeless** and still **lacks character**"* |
| one directional focused beam, source concealed | *"the artwork really **pops off the wall**… a clear and **intentional play between light and shadow**"* |
| a linear **wall grazer** at the base, skimming up | *"even more powerful… it's really **come to life**… depth, drama and an incredible wow factor"* |

Note that this is the same finding Nuno Silva reaches from the CG side
(`UmJaVnO3Sxw` `[01:01]`: *"if you have the sun facing both this front and the side you'll
not see correctly the dimensions"*). **A lighting director and an archviz critic, working
on completely different objects, both say the defect is UNIFORM INTENSITY.**

## 2. CONCEAL THE SOURCE — and this is P2r-29(b) answered exactly

`[04:43]` *"our eyes see the brightest thing first, so you must ensure that **sources of
light are well concealed**. For example, if you are lighting a shelving unit — the light
positioned in the underside of the shelf is always visible, and **with the light being the
brightest part of the unit, it's what you notice and you focus on.** But shouldn't you be
focusing on the **items displayed on the shelves** instead? Now you can see how, with
lighting concealed, our focus shifts to the objects on the shelves. **Concealing light is
what a lighting designer spends a lot of their time doing**, and it usually requires close
coordination with the architect, builder and **joinery workshop**."*

> C2's item on our casework strips at p2r84 was *"they glow but light nothing."* This is
> the same observation from the profession that owns it, with the fix attached: **the strip
> must stop being the bright thing so the contents can be.** P2r-29(b) is already filed as
> "LED strips sit in a REBATE instead of reading as raw bars on the soffit" — this is the
> outside authority for why the rebate is the point, and it also explains why it is a
> JOINERY change, not a lighting-parameter change.

And the fixture rule `[05:13]`: *"not all downlights and uplights are equal. What you're
looking for is a light source which is **baffled, i.e. recessed into the product**. **If the
source of light is flush on the ceiling or floor it's likely to be very glary**, and it's
the same case for a decorative light — you want to ensure the source of light is **shielded
from view** to maximise visual comfort."*

Read against the electrician's clip in this same curriculum (`t3F0dwsBFjw`, *"only as thick
as the drywall they mount into"*): **the FIXTURE is flush with the ceiling; the LAMP is
recessed up inside it.** Those are not in conflict, and our 60 mm-proud downlights satisfy
neither.

## 3. DECIDE WHAT NOT TO LIGHT

`[04:14]` *"as we enter a space **the eye is naturally drawn to the brightest point**. You
can take advantage of this involuntary response and use the power of light to **control
what is seen**. Think: what are the most important features in your space? This is where
you should be creating the most intense illumination. **The most challenging aspect of a
lighting design can often be deciding what NOT to light — by lighting too many features
you're at risk of having a washed-out effect.** So now you consider shadow as much as we do
light… **in this case less can often mean more.**"*

Our lighting work has only ever asked whether the frame is bright enough. It has never
asked which single feature should be the brightest, nor what should deliberately stay dark.

## 4. TWO NUMBERS, AND ONE HONEST CONTRADICTION TO RECORD

`[06:10]` *"for a **residential** environment a warm white colour between **2,200 and 2,700
Kelvin** is usually the most appropriate — it will give you a luxurious feel and works well
with our human response to lighting and its impact on our circadian rhythm. **But beware:
'warm white' is not specific enough to ensure consistent colour between different
suppliers.**"*

**This contradicts the other two sources in this curriculum**, and the contradiction is
worth keeping rather than averaging away:

| Source | Value | Context given |
|---|---|---|
| CCS Triangle Electric (`t3F0dwsBFjw` `[00:31]`) | **3000 K** | *"best for bedrooms, living rooms"* — US residential electrical practice |
| Nuno Silva (`UmJaVnO3Sxw` `[24:23]`) | **3000 K** | *"for the living areas it's a more comfortable light"* — archviz |
| John Cullen (this unit) | **2200-2700 K** | luxury residential lighting design, circadian argument |

The spread is real: a trade default at 3000 K, a luxury-design default up to 800 K warmer.
Our deliverable is judged against *delivered luxury residential work*, which argues for the
lower band — but that must be settled against the anchor pool, not by picking a favourite
video. It also has to be weighed against the electrician's brown-walls-read-pink warning,
which pushes the other way for a wood-dominant room.

`[06:39]` also licenses variety: *"whilst it's preferable to maintain the same colour
temperature between similar lights — for example across your range of downlights — **it is
acceptable and often desirable to mix colour temperatures if it's done in a methodical
way**; it can help offer a variety of lighting scenes and moods."*

## 5. LAYERS, AND WHEN LIGHTING IS DESIGNED

`[03:44]` *"the key will be to **layer** lighting rather than just using downlights… use a
combination of each of these. It's typically a balance between **ambient, task and accent**
lighting that's going to hit the spot. This layered approach gives a better aesthetic
result, also allowing flexibility to switch between different moods and functions."*

`[05:41]` **the sequencing rule, and this repo already learned it the hard way:** *"**if the
electrical contractor is already asking for lighting layouts, you've left it a bit late.**
You can start planning your lighting as soon as you have an idea of the **furniture
arrangements** and some idea of the **finishes** that will be used."*

That is our own recorded build order — dimension → objects → light — stated by the
profession: lighting is designed once furniture and finishes are known, and not before.

## 6. COMPOSITION (`sdIMItJ8sx8`)

Its opening line is the best statement of this whole curriculum's purpose `[00:00]`:
*"composition is one of those things that we usually naturally **feel that something is
wrong, but sometimes we just don't know what it is.**"* And `[00:29]`: *"**even if you don't
have great 3D skills, but you know and use the composition rules, your image can still be
successful. On the other hand the biggest skill set doesn't help you much to make your image
successful if your composition is off.**"*

The rules, each demonstrated on an interior render:

- **Rule of thirds** `[01:00]`, with the part usually left out: *"you cannot just simply put
  your focal point in one of the intersections and finish there. The best result will be
  achieved when you **balance it with something**"* — in his example, cosmetics bottom-left
  balanced by a bamboo plant opposite.
- Thirds can also divide **content**, not just points `[01:32]`: living room one third,
  kitchen two thirds; horizontally, shelves+ceiling / middle / island+floor.
- **Golden spiral** `[02:01]`, ending on a focal point; framing with a foreground element.
- **Human figures** `[03:01]`: *"human faces or figures in the image attract the viewer's
  eyes automatically… even the **look direction** of the character is important, because we
  naturally look in the same way the character is looking."* And at `[05:06]`: *"notice that
  she's turned into the centre of the image — **the image will look really weird if she
  will look the other way.**"*
- **Colour as attention** `[03:31]`: *"most of the image is blue or yellow but the focal
  point is **red**."*
- **Symmetry / visual weight** `[04:03]`: *"two things don't have to be exactly identical
  but they should have the **same visual weight** and balance on both sides."*
- **Golden triangle** `[05:38]` and **diagonals for dynamism** `[06:08]`.
- The disclaimer `[06:39]`: *"these are only guidelines… **artists should know them but
  should never see them as limitations**"*, and `[04:33]` *"if something looks better and
  it's not exactly on the line, always choose what feels better."*
- **BONUS, and it is a tool we could copy** `[07:11]`: a viewport **composition-helper
  overlay** used *"a lot during the **camera setup stage**"* — thirds, diagonals, golden
  ratio, golden triangle, golden spiral (rotatable), and a centre cross for symmetry, drawn
  over the live camera.

  It is a 3ds Max script, but the idea is software-independent and cheap: our camera work
  currently has no overlay at all, and `frame_geometry.py` already knows the frame.

## 7. WHAT IS OWED FROM THIS UNIT

1. **Stop treating the downlight grid as the lighting.** Layer it (ambient/task/accent),
   and name ONE feature that is the brightest thing in the frame.
2. **P2r-29(b)**: conceal the casework strip so the contents are the bright thing — a
   joinery rebate, coordinated with the carcass, not a shader value.
3. **Baffled fixtures**: the lamp recessed inside a flush housing; a flush source is glary.
4. Record the **2200-2700 K vs 3000 K** disagreement as an open question against the anchor
   pool, together with the brown-goes-pink caveat — do not silently average them.
5. **Decide what NOT to light**, and let the rest fall into shadow.
6. Build a **composition overlay** on our own camera (thirds + centre cross at minimum), and
   check the focal point has a counterweight rather than only sitting on a line.
