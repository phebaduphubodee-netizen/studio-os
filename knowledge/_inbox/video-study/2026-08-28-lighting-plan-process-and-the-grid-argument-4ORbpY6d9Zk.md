## 1. THE ARGUMENT, STATED HONESTLY — three sources, three positions, one agreement

This curriculum now holds three positions on downlight spacing, and they do NOT agree.
Averaging them would be the wrong move; the disagreement is the information.

| Source | Position |
|---|---|
| CCS Triangle Electric (`t3F0dwsBFjw` `[02:19]`) | **pitch ≤ 4 ft, ≥ 2 ft off the wall** — a trade default for even coverage |
| 30X40 (`4ORbpY6d9Zk` `[02:40]`) | **ceiling height ÷ 2**, *"a good rule of thumb"*, and he derives it |
| Liz (`qbZoZiZg6I4` `[02:17]`) | *"**divide the room ceiling height in half, that's how far apart they should be. Measure 3 ft out, 30 in out from the edges of the walls, light the corners — it's all nonsense.**"* |

**What all three actually agree on** — and this is the load-bearing part:

- 30X40 `[01:46]`: *"what I don't want to do is create an **even grid of fixtures across the
  whole ceiling**… the thing that makes the forest beautiful is the **contrast between
  lights and darks**."*
- 30X40 `[00:00]`: *"designing the lighting is **not about evenly lighting spaces**, it's
  developing **contrast** — that difference between light and dark, that's the thing that
  makes you feel something in a space."*
- Liz `[02:49]`: *"**contractors, electricians in particular, are really fond of the grid**,
  and there's a reason for that: the lights run straight down one bay, it's not complicated.
  **That's not what we're aiming for.**"*
- John Cullen (`aRWwOhjbvfs` `[03:16]`): *"please no more grids of downlights."*

> **The resolution: the formula is a default for UNIFORM AMBIENT COVERAGE, and uniform
> ambient coverage is not the goal.** A spacing rule answers "how do I light a floor
> evenly"; nobody in this curriculum thinks an evenly lit floor is a designed room. Our
> twelve-point grid is the answer to a question a designer does not ask.
>
> And 30X40's derivation `[02:40]` shows why even the formula is not a constant: *"if we
> look at a section, a recessed light here and a recessed light here casts a cone of light…
> as the ceiling height gets taller, if we kept the same spacing we're effectively
> **over-lighting** the floor space, because this beam overlaps this beam — so we can
> actually **increase** the spacing for taller ceilings. Of course that's going to be
> informed by the **actual light fixture** that you choose."*

Liz's version of the same dependency `[06:08]`: *"**you can't possibly know how many lights
you're going to need until you know the specifications of the light that you're going to
get.**"* — the count is DERIVED from the fixture, never chosen first. That is the same shape
as this repo's own placement law: a number that can be derived must never be typed.

## 2. THE PROCESS, IN ORDER (30X40)

1. `[00:00]` **Sketch fixture layouts by TYPE first** — *"when I say fixture I mean: is it a
   pendant, is it a recessed light, is it a wall sconce. We're going to determine what
   fixture types we're going to use where first, and then we'll take this into the computer
   and we'll do the **circuiting and switching** diagrams."*
2. `[00:52]` **Know the ceiling planes and the furniture layout** — *"where your flat
   ceiling planes are, where your pitched ceiling planes are, and also the furniture
   layout… all these things inform the **pattern of use**, and that's a great way to begin
   thinking about the lighting plan."*
3. `[01:46]` **Three types**: ambient (general level), task, accent (*"used to highlight
   features in the architecture that we want to call attention to"*).
4. `[08:47]` **Coordinate with structure before it is frozen** — *"if we want to be locating
   recessed lights in this roof plane, or we want a junction box for power to supply our
   pendant fixtures… we need to plan for all these things **before we finalise the
   structural design**. I've had this happen many times before, where we didn't overlay the
   lighting plan on the structural plan, and you want this bank of lights centred in this
   hallway but **there's a framing member right there.**"*

Liz's version of the inputs `[03:54]`-`[05:33]`: gather plans → **ceiling obstructions**
(beams, coffers, sprinklers, other fixtures) → **ceiling height** (*"an 8 ft ceiling and a
12 ft ceiling are going to need different specifications"*) → furniture → **traffic** →
**task surfaces** (*"the kitchen countertop versus the coffee table — they're going to have
different demands for light"*) → then choose fixtures → then count.

**Drawing convention** `[00:52]`: *"the floor plan is kind of **greyed out**, the walls are
greyed out — the most important information in a lighting plan are the **fixtures and the
switching**, so those things will be more prominent on the page, and I usually do them in
**red**."*

## 3. FIVE MOVES THAT APPLY TO OUR ROOM

### 3.1 THE CEILING AS A LUMINOUS PLANE — the answer to a flat ceiling with no source
`[07:02]` *"one of the things I was thinking about was **how do we fill the volume of this
space**, and maybe treat the ceiling similarly to how we're treating these wall surfaces —
that we could have this **ceiling act as a sort of luminous plane**. So what if we had some
**adjustable mono points** that are really starting to **wash this ceiling** with light —
that really creates then a nice surface, and **if this is a wood ceiling this creates a nice
ambient light surface to light up the room.**"*

> Our ceiling is measured flat at a 0.73 gradient with no light source in frame, and the
> lane's answer has always been to change an amount. This is a different answer: **make the
> ceiling a lit SURFACE, washed from below**, so that it becomes the ambient source instead
> of a grey lid with holes in it. And he says it works especially well over wood.

### 3.2 GIMBALS, NOT FIXED DOWNLIGHTS
`[03:34]` *"here I've chosen a **gimbal** light fixture, so this recessed is able to be
twisted and turned, has a full degree of rotation, so we can actually take this light and
aim it over here… I like this level of **adjustability**… start to use it to really create
these kind of **luminous planes**."* Liz `[06:40]` agrees: *"they may not all be the same
type of recessed light — some might be a downlight, some might have a **gimbal** so it
adjusts and hits artwork or cabinetry."*

**Our twelve fixtures are all one type, all aimed straight down.** A real plan mixes.

### 3.3 DO NOT LIGHT EVERY OBJECT
`[04:27]` *"I think you want to **resist this tendency to really light and centre on every
object**. You know, think about being in the tub — **you don't actually want to be on
stage** necessarily. So I think lighting the **spaces surrounding** it is kind of nice."*

Third source for "decide what not to light" (after John Cullen and Andy Christoforou).

### 3.4 A WINDOW AT NIGHT IS A BLACK PLANE
`[09:41]` *"in this space here at night, **each one of these windows is going to be a black
plane** if we don't do something on the outside face — so what I'm going to propose is that
we do some sort of **in-deck light**."*

A frame-level fact our lane has never tested. Whatever our glazing does at the frame's
exposure, it either shows something outside or it reads as a hole. Both are choices; ours
is not currently a choice.

### 3.5 CENTRE FIXTURES ON THE ARCHITECTURE
`[08:47]` *"let's just **centre it on these windows** — it should make sense"*, and the
sconces *"centred over each one of these windows… so that bank can then light and create
this luminous plane."* Fixtures are aligned to architectural elements, not to a grid
spacing.

## 4. FIXTURE SPECIFICATION — the properties Liz says you must know before counting

`[05:33]`-`[06:40]`: **lumens · colour temperature · beam spread · CRI.**

- **CRI ≥ 85**: *"you want that to be somewhere **85 or better**, unless it's some crappy
  accent light. You want good quality light to show off the colours and the finishes that
  you picked… **if you don't have it, it's going to change the quality of those beautiful
  finishes.**"*

  Directly relevant: our room's whole case rests on wood colour, and this is the second
  warning in the curriculum that the source can change how a finish reads (the first being
  brown-goes-pink at 3000 K, `t3F0dwsBFjw`).
- **Beam spread trades against quality**: *"you need **less lights** when you have a wider
  beam spread; you also get **less quality light**, because the light is sort of diffused
  out."*
- **Switching groups** `[07:13]`: which fixtures come on together, and where the switches
  are relative to how you enter and leave the room.

And the flat prohibition `[07:45]`:
> *"**never ever use just one source, one type. One. Never. Just don't do it**… there will
> be other lights besides the recessed lights in a room… **they are an addition, they are
> not the be-all and end-all. They are not your only source of light, and if they are, your
> lighting plan will suffer.**"*

Our room's ambient is twelve identical recessed fixtures and nothing else.

## 5. WHAT IS OWED FROM THIS UNIT

1. **Stop defending the grid's spacing and replace the grid's PURPOSE.** Uniform floor
   coverage is not the goal; contrast is. Record the three-way disagreement (§1) as it
   stands rather than picking one number.
2. **Try the ceiling as a washed luminous plane** (§3.1) — it is the only proposal in the
   curriculum that answers "bright with no source" without adding a visible fixture to the
   frame.
3. **Mix fixture types and aim them** (§3.2); at minimum, some gimbals aimed at the wardrobe
   wall rather than twelve straight-down cans.
4. **Decide what not to light** (§3.3) — third source.
5. **Test the glazing at night / at frame exposure** (§3.4): does it show anything, or is it
   a black plane?
6. **Derive the count from the fixture spec** (lumens, beam, CRI ≥ 85), never from a grid;
   and never ship a room whose only source is one fixture type.
