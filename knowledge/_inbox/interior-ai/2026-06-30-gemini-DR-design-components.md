## EVIDENCE / FINDINGS — substantive content, organized by topic, with the authoritative textbook/standard named for each block.

### Phase 1: Programming and Client Brief

The Programming phase is the foundational information-gathering stage. Its goal is to understand the client's needs, goals, and the project's constraints before any design work begins. This is a crucial analytical, rather than creative, step.

*   **Objective**: To produce a comprehensive written document that outlines the entire scope of the project. This "program" serves as the textual blueprint and point of reference for all subsequent design decisions.
*   **Key Activities**: Client interviews, user questionnaires, site analysis (measuring and documenting existing conditions), inventory of existing furniture/equipment, budget analysis, and regulatory code review (zoning, building codes, accessibility standards).
*   **Authoritative Source**: *Space Planning Basics* by Karlen and Fleming is a primary authority on this phase. It defines programming as the process of gathering and analyzing information about the problem to be solved. *Human Dimension & Interior Space* by Panero and Zelnik provides the essential anthropometric and ergonomic data required to translate human needs into spatial requirements during this phase.
*   **Professional Deliverable: The Interior Design Program**
    *   **Goals and Objectives**: A summary of the client's primary motivations and desired outcomes.
    *   **User Profiles**: Description of the primary and secondary users of the space (e.g., family members, employees, customers).
    *   **Spatial Requirements**: A list of all required spaces and their approximate sizes (net square footage/meterage).
    *   **Adjacency Requirements**: A matrix or diagram showing which spaces need to be near each other and which should be separate.
    *   **Functional Requirements**: Detailed descriptions of the activities to be performed in each space, including furniture, equipment, and storage needs.
    *   **Technical Requirements**: Acoustical, lighting, and specific power/data requirements for each space.
    *   **Budget and Schedule**: A preliminary budget and project timeline.
    *   **Code Synopsis**: A summary of applicable building codes, energy codes, and accessibility standards (e.g., ADA, IBC).

### Phase 2: Schematic Design (or Conceptual Design)

In this phase, the abstract requirements from the program are translated into broad, conceptual design solutions. The focus is on the overall design intent, spatial relationships, and general aesthetic, not fine detail.

*   **Objective**: To develop and receive client approval on a basic design direction and space plan.
*   **Key Activities**: Developing bubble diagrams and adjacency diagrams, creating blocking and stacking plans (for multi-level projects), sketching initial floor plan concepts (schematic plans), and creating mood boards or concept imagery.
*   **Authoritative Source**: *Interior Design Illustrated* by Francis D.K. Ching is the definitive guide for translating programmatic concepts into visual form. It provides the graphic conventions and foundational principles for drawing plans, sections, and understanding spatial relationships. *Space Planning Basics* by Karlen and Fleming outlines the systematic process of moving from diagrams to initial floor plans.
*   **Professional Deliverables**:
    *   **Bubble/Adjacency Diagrams**: Freehand diagrams illustrating the location and relative importance of spaces and their connections.
    *   **Schematic Floor Plans**: Preliminary, often single-line drawings showing the arrangement of rooms, circulation paths, and major furniture/equipment. These establish scale and proportion.
    *   **Conceptual Sketches/Renderings**: Simple 3D views (perspectives, axonometrics) or digital massing models to convey the spatial volume and character.
    *   **Mood/Concept Boards**: Collections of images, materials, and colors that establish the intended aesthetic direction.

### Phase 3: Design Development

This phase refines the approved schematic design into a detailed and resolved plan. Decisions are made about specific materials, finishes, furniture, and fixtures, and the design is developed with greater precision.

*   **Objective**: To finalize all major design decisions and produce a set of drawings and specifications that are detailed enough for preliminary cost estimating and client sign-off before the final construction documentation phase.
*   **Key Activities**: Refining floor plans with specific wall thicknesses, selecting all materials and finishes, choosing specific furniture, fixtures, and equipment (FF&E), designing custom millwork/built-ins, and developing the lighting plan.
*   **Authoritative Source**: *Interiors: An Introduction* by Nielson and Taylor provides a comprehensive overview of the elements that are refined in this stage, including materials, FF&E, and color theory. *Materials for Interior Environments* by Cindy Binggeli is the authoritative text for the technical properties, applications, and standards related to interior materials. For lighting, the *IES Lighting Handbook* and Gary Gordon's *Interior Lighting for Designers* are the definitive standards, providing recommended light levels (foot-candles or lux) and quality metrics (e.g., Color Rendering Index - CRI) for different tasks and environments.
*   **Professional Deliverables**:
    *   **Developed Floor Plans**: Dimensionally-correct plans showing partitions, doors, windows, millwork, major fixtures, and keyed references to other drawings.
    *   **Reflected Ceiling Plans (RCPs)**: Drawings showing the location of all ceiling elements: light fixtures, diffusers, sprinkler heads, smoke detectors, and any changes in ceiling height or material.
    *   **Key Elevations**: Interior elevations of significant walls, showing vertical dimensions, millwork design, and the placement of outlets, switches, and wall-mounted fixtures.
    *   **Character Renderings/Visualizations**: More detailed 3D models or photorealistic renderings to accurately communicate the look and feel of the space, including materials and lighting.
    *   **Material and Finish Schedule**: A tabular list specifying the exact materials for floors, walls, ceilings, etc., for each room (e.g., Floor-1: XYZ Brand, LVT, Model #123).
    *   **FF&E Selections**: A presentation binder or digital document with images and cut sheets for all proposed furniture, fabrics, and equipment.
    *   **Preliminary FF&E Budget**: An itemized cost estimate for the FF&E package.

### Phase 4: Construction Documentation

This is the most technical phase, where the approved design is translated into a comprehensive set of drawings and specifications that will be used for bidding, permitting, and construction. The goal is to eliminate ambiguity and provide the builder with exact instructions.

*   **Objective**: To produce a complete, legally binding set of documents that clearly communicates the design intent and construction requirements to the general contractor.
*   **Authoritative Source**: *Interior Design Illustrated* by Ching is the foundational text for learning how to create these drawings with correct graphic standards and conventions. Professional practice manuals and building code publications (like the International Building Code - IBC) are also essential references.
*   **Professional Deliverables: The Construction Document Set (CDs)**
    *   **Cover Sheet**: Project name, address, drawing index, code information.
    *   **Construction Plans**: Dimensioned floor plans showing demolition, new partitions, power/data locations, and keynotes.
    *   **Reflected Ceiling Plans (RCPs)**: Fully dimensioned and annotated with all ceiling elements and switching information.
    *   **Elevations**: Detailed interior elevations for all walls with millwork, tile layouts, or complex features.
    *   **Sections**: Drawings cutting through key parts of the building or millwork to show construction, material layers, and relationships between components.
    *   **Details**: Large-scale drawings of specific construction connections and assemblies (e.g., how a custom countertop meets a wall, a stair railing detail).
    *   **Schedules**:
        *   **Door Schedule**: Lists every door by number, with its type, size, material, finish, and hardware set.
        *   **Finish Schedule**: A matrix specifying the exact finish for the floor, base, walls (and often wainscot/accent walls), and ceiling of every room.
        *   **Lighting Fixture Schedule**: Lists every fixture type with a tag, image/symbol, manufacturer, model number, lamping information, and voltage.
    *   **Specifications ("Specs")**: A separate written volume (often referencing CSI MasterFormat) that provides a detailed, technical description of the quality, workmanship, and installation requirements for all materials and products. This document is legally binding.

### Phase 5: Bidding and Negotiation (or Tendering)

This phase involves getting bids from contractors and selecting one to build the project.

*   **Objective**: To solicit competitive bids from qualified contractors and award a construction contract.
*   **Key Activities**: Issuing the construction documents to multiple contractors, responding to Requests for Information (RFIs), analyzing the received bids, and assisting the client in negotiating and signing a contract.
*   **Deliverables**: Addenda (clarifications issued during bidding), bid analysis matrix, and the final construction contract between client and contractor.

### Phase 6: Contract Administration (or Construction Administration)

The designer's role during construction is to ensure the project is built according to the construction documents.

*   **Objective**: To represent the client's interests during construction, verify conformance with the design intent, and manage project changes.
*   **Key Activities**: Attending site meetings, reviewing contractor submittals (shop drawings, material samples), responding to RFIs, issuing clarification sketches or change orders, and creating punch lists (a list of deficiencies to be corrected) at the end of the project.
*   **Deliverables**: Field reports, reviewed submittals, responses to RFIs, punch lists, and project closeout documentation.

## CANONICAL SOURCES — a bulleted list of the specific books/standards a builder should acquire, with author + title (+ edition/year if known) and what each is the authority FOR.

*   **Francis D.K. Ching, *Interior Design Illustrated*** (any recent edition): **THE authority for graphic standards.** It teaches how to draw and, more importantly, *read* all standard interior design drawings (plans, elevations, sections, details). It is the essential visual dictionary for design documentation.
*   **Mark Karlen & Christina Fleming, *Space Planning Basics*** (any recent edition): **THE authority for the programming and schematic design process.** It provides a logical, step-by-step methodology for translating client needs into functional, code-compliant floor plans.
*   **Julius Panero & Martin Zelnik, *Human Dimension & Interior Space: A Source Book of Design Reference Standards***: **THE authority for anthropometrics and ergonomics.** This book contains the essential data on human body sizes and spatial requirements for movement and activities. It is the basis for dimensioning clearances, circulation paths, and workstations correctly.
*   **Karla J. Nielson & David A. Taylor, *Interiors: An Introduction***: A comprehensive **textbook-grade overview of the entire profession**, covering principles and elements of design, materials, FF&E, and professional practice. It connects all the pieces.
*   **Cindy Binggeli, *Materials for Interior Environments***: **THE authority for the technical specifications of materials.** It goes beyond aesthetics to cover performance characteristics, sustainability, and installation methods for everything from flooring to wall coverings.
*   **Illuminating Engineering Society (IES), *The IES Lighting Handbook*** (most recent edition): **THE definitive legal and professional standard for lighting design.** It provides the required quantitative data (e.g., illuminance levels in lux/footcandles) for all types of spaces and tasks, as well as qualitative best practices.
*   **Gary Gordon, *Interior Lighting for Designers***: A more **accessible, design-focused guide to lighting** that translates the dense engineering principles of the IES Handbook into practical application for designers. It's excellent for understanding *how* to achieve lighting effects.

## SYNTHESIS — an integrated, builder-usable answer to the original question.

A professional interior design project is a structured, multi-phase process designed to move from an abstract idea to a fully documented and constructed reality in a predictable way. For a builder, understanding these phases and the deliverables from each is critical for accurate bidding, efficient project management, and delivering the client's expected outcome without ambiguity.

The process flows sequentially through six distinct phases:

1.  **Programming**: This is the research phase. The designer produces a written "Program" that is the project's rulebook, defining every client need, required space, function, and technical constraint. For you, this document is the source of truth for *why* the design is the way it is.

2.  **Schematic Design**: This is the "big idea" phase. The designer takes the written program and creates initial diagrams and simple floor plans (schematics) that show the basic layout and design concept. These are not for construction but for getting client approval on the direction.

3.  **Design Development**: This is the detailing phase. The approved schematic plan is refined with specific dimensions, materials, finishes, furniture, lighting fixtures, and custom-built elements. The output is a set of more detailed drawings, schedules, and renderings that represent a complete picture of the final design. This is the stage where you could provide preliminary cost estimates.

4.  **Construction Documentation**: This is the most critical phase for you as the builder. The designer produces the formal, legal "Construction Documents" (CDs). This is a comprehensive set of drawings (plans, elevations, sections, details) and written specifications. **These documents are not a suggestion; they are a set of instructions.** They are intended to be precise enough to build from without requiring the designer to be on-site 24/7. Key components you will always see are:
    *   **Plans**: Overhead views with precise dimensions. The Reflected Ceiling Plan (RCP) is your map for all ceiling work.
    *   **Elevations & Sections**: Vertical views that show heights, millwork construction, and material transitions.
    *   **Details**: Zoomed-in drawings of complex junctions, showing exactly how components are to be assembled.
    *   **Schedules**: Tables that efficiently list every door, window, finish, and light fixture, cross-referenced to the plans. This prevents cluttering the drawings.
    *   **Specifications (Specs)**: The written rulebook for quality. It defines the required material standards, installation methods, and testing protocols. It takes precedence over the drawings in case of a conflict.

5.  **Bidding/Tendering**: The designer assists the client in selecting you, the builder, based on the completed Construction Documents.

6.  **Construction Administration**: Once you are building, the designer's role is to observe construction to ensure it conforms to the documents, answer your questions (RFIs), and approve material samples (submittals). They act as the client's representative to protect the integrity of the design.

## ACTIONABLE FOR AN AI DRAFTING PIPELINE — concrete elements/fields/checks this implies for a spec-driven generator.

To create a "complete" professional deliverable, an AI pipeline must be built on a structured data model that mirrors these phases and components.

**1. Input Data Model (Based on Programming):**
*   **Project Info**: Client, address, project type, scope.
*   **Code Data**: Applicable IBC version, local amendments, ADA requirements.
*   **Room Data Structure (for each room)**:
    *   `Room_Name`: (e.g., "Primary Bedroom")
    *   `Room_ID`: (e.g., "101")
    *   `Required_SF/M2`: (e.g., 250 sq. ft.)
    *   `Adjacency_Requirements`: (List of `Room_ID`s with `Proximity` flag: "Immediate", "Near", "Separate")
    *   `Functional_Requirements`: (Text field or tags: "Sleep", "Reading", "Dressing")
    *   `Key_Furniture_Items`: (List: "King Bed", "2x Nightstand", "Dresser")
    *   `Lighting_Requirements`: (Task: "Reading", Ambient, Accent; `IES_Lux_Target`: 500 lux at task plane)
    *   `Acoustic_Requirements`: (STC rating for partitions)
    *   `Power_Data_Reqs`: (Number of outlets, data port locations)

**2. Generation Rules & Logic (Schematic & Design Development):**
*   **Space Planning Module**: Must use rules from *Space Planning Basics* and *Human Dimension & Interior Space*.
    *   **Circulation Check**: `Rule: MIN_CLEARANCE_HALLWAY = 36 inches` (or 44" for commercial). `Rule: MIN_CLEARANCE_DOORWAY = 32 inches`. These must be non-violable constraints.
    *   **Adjacency Solver**: An algorithm to optimize room placement based on the input adjacency matrix.
*   **FF&E & Material Assignment**:
    *   The system must link to a structured database of real-world products.
    *   **FF&E Object**: `Manufacturer`, `Model_Number`, `Dimensions (L,W,H)`, `Material`, `Finish_Code`.
    *   **Material Object**: `Manufacturer`, `Product_Line`, `SKU`, `Performance_Specs` (e.g., abrasion rating for fabric, slip resistance for tile). Data structure should follow formats from Binggeli's *Materials for Interior Environments*.

**3. Output Generation (Construction Documents):**
The AI must be able to generate a complete, cross-referenced CD set. This implies the system isn't just "drawing pictures" but is building a relational database of the project.

*   **Completeness Checklists**: Before outputting, the AI must run a validation check:
    *   *Is every room assigned a finish in the Finish Schedule?*
    *   *Does every symbol on the Lighting Plan have a corresponding entry in the Lighting Fixture Schedule?*
    *   *Is every door on the plan listed in the Door Schedule?*
    *   *Are all custom millwork elements detailed in an elevation and/or section view?*
    *   *Are all plans dimensioned?* (Check for dangling dimension strings).
*   **Automated Annotation**:
    *   Generate keynote bubbles automatically linked to a keynote database.
    *   Place door, window, and room tags automatically, pulling data from the underlying project model.
*   **Schedule Generation**:
    *   The AI should query its internal project database to automatically generate all schedules (Finish, Lighting, Door). This ensures the drawings and schedules are never out of sync.
*   **Specifications Link**:
    *   The system should be able to link materials specified on the drawings to corresponding sections in a CSI MasterFormat-based specification document. For example, a "PT-1" paint tag on a wall in an elevation should link to Spec Section 09 91 00 - Painting.

By structuring the AI pipeline around these authoritative phases, data requirements, and validation checks, the output can move from being a mere visual representation to a true, professional-grade, and buildable set of instructions.
