## 1. Surface vignettes (nightstand · dresser · bench · shelf bay)

- **Odd rule with surface mapping [CITED — MontCarta "3-5-7 Rule", Tidbits & Twine]:**
  3 items = constrained surfaces (nightstands, small pedestals, compact shelf bays) ·
  5 = medium (dressers, credenzas, coffee tables, mantels) · 7 = expansive (long tables, multi-bay
  shelving). Escape valve when a grouping feels heavy: *remove the smallest item*.
- **Symmetry legitimately overrides** the odd rule for: flanking pairs at the bed (lamps/nightstands),
  formal/traditional and Chinese/Peranakan registers, strict minimalism (single statement piece).
  Real-estate staging PREFERS symmetric bedsides [ask-04 §4, Emily Henderson reader thread].
- **Decorative triangle [CITED — MontCarta, Creek Line House, Kylie M]:** tallest at back = anchor;
  second-largest beside it; smallest in front. Heights must vary — a flat skyline reads unresolved.
  - Medium element ≈ **½–⅔ of the tall element** [CITED — MontCarta states "half to two-thirds"].
  - Low element = "a flat or very short item" that grounds the group (book stack, tray, stone)
    [CITED as words]; the 0.15–0.25× ratio is **[SYNTH]** — our own translation to a number.
- **Depth layering [1SRC — CB2, order-of-operations]:** place largest pieces first (X-pattern across a
  grid), fill with medium, add small last. Rear = tall/flat leaned pieces; front = trays/low platforms
  carrying small detailed objects [SYNTH structure; the CB2 sequence is the cited part].
- **Contrast footprints:** never two identical-height / identical-base neighbours — wide round beside
  thin angular beside rectilinear stack [CITED as principle across MontCarta/Tip Top].
- **How full may a surface be:** occupied ≈ **40% of the surface, 60% exposed** [1SRC — Roomtodo
  "60/40 rule", the only independent quantified line]. The per-surface lived-in/staged vacancy table
  (nightstand 30–40/60–70% …) is **[SYNTH]** — use as bracketing start values only.

## 2. Wall art — sizing & hanging [CITED ×3 each unless noted]

- Width = **⅔–¾ of the furniture below** (headboard/console); never wider than the furniture.
  Art on a bare wall: 40–60% of active wall width [SYNTH-leaning; main answer only].
- Centre of art at **57–60″ = 1448–1524 mm** from floor (bare walls, museum standard);
  seated zones drop to 40–44″ = 1016–1118 mm [1SRC].
- Bottom edge **6–10″ = 152–254 mm above the headboard**; above dresser 6–8″ = 152–203 mm
  (4–6″ if tall surface objects overlap the art) [1SRC for the overlap variant].
- Multi-piece sets: uniform gaps — 2–3″ (51–76 mm) small frames · 4–5″ (102–127 mm) large;
  gap >6″ (152 mm) breaks the set into separate pieces [SYNTH-leaning; not re-verified].
- **Sources split (declared open):** headboards >48″ tall — skip art vs thin panoramic near ceiling.
- Bedroom-register subject/frame bounds → §6 matrix (calm horizontals; busy geometric abstracts
  prohibited over the bed [1SRC]).

## 3. Bedroom rugs [CITED ×3–4 each]

- Extend **18–24″ = 457–610 mm beyond bed sides and foot**. <18″ reads skimpy/accidental;
  >30″ (762 mm) overwhelms a small room.
- Keep **12–18″ = 305–457 mm bare floor to walls**; compact urban rooms may drop to ~8″ = 203 mm.
- Three layouts: **fully-under** (nightstands + bench on the rug; king → 9×12–10×14 ft, queen →
  8×10–9×12 ft) · **two-thirds-under** (most common; rug starts 6–12″ = 152–305 mm in front of the
  nightstands, nightstands on bare floor) · **runners** (2×6/2×8 ft flanking; wider than the
  nightstand, never past the foot).
- **Foot bench changes the rule:** rug runs ≥12″ = 305 mm past the bench front [1SRC states 12″;
  a second source confirms direction only]; luxury register 24–36″. Bench all four legs on the rug
  [1SRC — Aosom]. Can't reach it → size the rug up a class, don't slide it.
- **Contested (both camps documented, neither wins):** nightstands ON the rug (hotel island) vs OFF
  (two-thirds camp). Our spec's asymmetric two-thirds-style rug is inside documented practice.

## 4. Whole-room completeness — there is NO cited total count

**No independent source states a room-wide object count** (asked directly; unit `ask-06`). Any
whole-room threshold (e.g. "≥12 loose objects") is a house number, not literature. Sources are
per-zone; a complete styled bedroom =

| zone | staged / restrained | editorial / lived-warm |
|---|---|---|
| bed pillows | 2–4 | 5–6 (2/person sleep + 3 euro + optional lumbar) [CITED] |
| bedding layers | 3 | up to 5 incl. foot throw [CITED] |
| each nightstand | 2–3 ("lamp, book, glass") | 4 [CITED] |
| dresser top | 3 (tray · mirror · few frames) | 5–6 (lamp, tray, frames, tall vase w/ branches) [1SRC ea.] |
| seating nook | chair + lamp + table, +1 cushion +1 throw [1SRC formula] | same, throw pooling |
| floor | 1 rug (§3) | may layer/offset |
| walls | 1 statement piece or 2–3-piece set | same, more personal |
| windows | sheer + blackout double layer [1SRC] | same |
| plants | 1 | 2 (one trailing high, one sculptural mid) [SYNTH counts; species CITED] |

- Density fails BOTH ways, quotably: bare shelves/empty tables = "challenge ANY interior to sing"
  (sterile), and the Emily Henderson over-styling reversal ("liked it more when it was WAYY more
  paired back") = clutter. Keep surfaces "clean but not empty".

## 5. Open-shelf grid grammar (the "empty shelves / staged bays" answer)

- **≥25% of every shelf surface empty**; airy-minimal register: books ≤ ⅓ of shelf space, one
  significant object per section [1SRC — Tip Top, concrete ratios].
- **X-pattern / diagonal distribution [CITED — CB2, Creek Line House]:** stagger same-size/shape/
  colour pieces diagonally across the grid, never stacked in the same column; largest placed first.
- **Identical-item bounds [1SRC — Creek Line House]:** 2 identical = bold/modern · 3-in-a-row =
  peaceful/quaint · **>3 = retail merchandising, prohibited.** (Consistent with our max-2-instances
  per asset id in frame — ORD-25c amendment already stricter.)

## 6. Story → object list (ORD-2026-08-25c steps 1–3, now grounded)

Five DOCUMENTED methods (verbatim quotes in unit `ask-04`):
1. **Script breakdown** (Peerspace, film set dressing): scene-by-scene notes → itemized dressing list;
   socioeconomics + interests of the character pick material quality and subjects; period/geography research.
2. **Props list + runsheet** (MIT Theater Guild): rough list from the script → director alignment →
   prop/set/costume boundary talk → runsheet mapping item↔scene↔character↔status → cull ~25% on
   condition/durability/reusability.
3. **E-commerce room-set storytelling** (Prodoto): choose characters → style follows character →
   plant story cues (open book, cup on side table) → mood-board-aligned colour scheme.
4. **Persona-driven FF&E kits** (Ark & Mason, Airbnb tier): 2–3 persona profiles → priorities →
   spec sheets with SKUs + sourcing links; stage for the 3 best camera angles, then strip what crowds frame.
5. **Mood-board motif analysis** (Kevin Francis): 30–50 reference images → recurring motifs (colour
   temp, era, texture, scale) → name ONE anchor-object archetype → test in mock layout.
- **ABSENT from literature (cited absence):** which bed side belongs to which resident → stays a
  story decision we author. Natural-asymmetry devices that ARE documented: nightstands harmonious in
  style/scale/finish rather than identical; align art bottom edges even over unequal tables; round rug
  under one bed corner for asymmetric layouts.

- **Plant size classes [1SRC bands, usable with citation]:** floor 900–1500+ mm tall / pot Ø300–450 mm ·
  tabletop 300–600 / Ø150–250 · shelf-trailing 150–300 / Ø100–150.
- **Cushion sizes on seating [1SRC]:** armchair = one 550×550 statement OR large square + 300×500
  lumbar; bench = 2 cushions on ONE side, remaining ⅔ bare or throw-draped; throw covers ⅓–½ of a
  bench, diagonal, folded lengthwise in thirds; drape = hold centre, let fall, scrunch — never pressed.
  Throw/cushion sits several shades off the upholstery; contrast texture (smooth chair ↔ rough throw).
- **Library-filtering workflows [each 1SRC]:** 80/20 style blending (80% in-style, 20% personality
  accents — bounds how many off-style pieces may enter) · 70/20/10 colour proportioning ·
  mood-board motif analysis (§6.5) · prop-vetting cull questions (condition / durability / reusability).

### 8.1 A SIXTH METHOD FOR §6 — OBSERVED, NOT DOCUMENTED

§6 lists five DOCUMENTED story→object methods. This adds a sixth of a different kind: not a
method anyone wrote down, but **an invariant observed across three designers handed the same
bedroom photograph**, plus a fourth working four real bedrooms.

**first impression → story/vision sentence → FLOOR → WALLS → storage → bed and its
orientation → the rest of the furniture → LIGHTING LAST.**

- All three state a **story sentence before naming a single object**, and two of the three
  derive it from **the person who will live there** rather than from a style name.
- Lighting does not appear in any of the three until the final minutes. A lighting director
  and an architect drawing lighting plans both independently say the same thing — light is
  designed once furniture and finishes are known.
- The fourth designer's per-room order is: whole-room verdict → **layout/orientation** → the
  basics (cords, storage, mess) → **a focal point** → soft goods → colour last, derived from
  something already in the room. His own words for the third step: *"attack the basics first
  before we really get into the fun things."*

> **What this adds to §6 in practice:** §6's methods all produce an object LIST. This produces
> an ORDER, and the order says the list is not the first artefact — the story sentence is,
> and the surfaces are settled before the objects.

### 8.2 A FOCAL POINT IS A REQUIRED PART, NOT A NICETY
Four independent sources name it as something a room must have and can lack: *"clearly, her
room needs a sort of focal point"*, *"I think what I really want to do is create a focal
point"*, *"it's very important to create a strong focal point"* (a visual-merchandising
lecturer), and an archviz reviewer testing every image by where the eye lands.

§4 records that there is NO cited total object count for a finished room. A focal point is the
nearest thing to a completeness test that the practitioners actually use: **not how many
objects, but whether one of them is the subject.**

### 8.3 THE VOCABULARY — named defects a report can cite
Collected because a builder that cannot NAME a defect cannot report one. Every phrase is a
whole-room judgement made in under five words with the room visible.

| Phrase | What it judges |
|---|---|
| *"quite a bit of clutter"* | object count / surface discipline |
| *"it feels like a closet"* | circulation, not decoration |
| *"a hallway that leads to kind of nowhere"* | leftover floor with no purpose |
| *"a train of furniture"* | pieces in an unbroken line — *"let's try and just put some space between things"* |
| *"plastered against the wall"* | no gap behind a piece — *"we've got to give it some wiggle room"* |
| *"every wall completely covered in furniture"* | no **breathing room** |
| *"all flat things on the wall — that falls flat"* | one KIND of object repeated; also *"when it's just all shelves everywhere"* |
| *"it's just really, really boring"* | no focal point, no contrast |
| *"everything is very hard"* / *"very rigid"* | absence of soft goods |
| *"the room feels cold"* | see `knowledge/styles/color-composition.md` §14.3 |
| *"it's almost like it's not done"* | too simple, too empty, no context |

**Use limit (R7d):** these are for the builder to REPORT with, never to convict with. A defect
enters the ledger from C2, C3 or the owner.

### 8.4 OBJECTS ARE HABITUALLY CHOSEN TOO SMALL — four sources
Two designers agreeing unprompted about the same object class: *"everybody goes too small on
their bedside table lamps. If whatever size you pick, it's too small."* — *"go like two sizes
bigger. I promise you."* A third states it at room scale: *"let each element be large and
unapologetic — it could be the entire length of that wall."* A fourth specifies deliberately
oversized pendants that a collaborating architect called an error, and stands by it.

And why it is not a trivial finishing item: *"having the correct bowl or the correct vase
almost feels like a frivolous thing, because it's not the sofa and it's not the rug… but it
ridiculously really can tie the space together as the finishing piece. It's kind of like
having the tux without the bow tie. **It IS actually a focal point.**"*

§1's vignette rules say what goes on a surface; this says the pieces are probably a size or
two under, and that the error is systematic rather than occasional.

### 8.5 A PROPORTION CONSTANT FOR ANYTHING MOUNTED ABOVE SOMETHING
*"the fireplace is the exact same width as the TV — to me that's the killer… speaking in the
constants of design proportions, ideally a TV up there would be a little bit **smaller**. It
ends up feeling kind of overwhelming; it won't feel balanced."*

**A mounted object must be narrower than the element it sits on.** §2 sizes art to the wall
and to the furniture below; this adds the failure state when the two are equal.

### 8.6 THE CONTESTED RUG ITEM IN §3 GETS A THIRD VOICE, WITH A REASON
§3 records the nightstands-ON versus nightstands-OFF camps as contested with neither winning.
A designer states the ON position and gives a use-based reason rather than a styling one:

> *"There's nothing I hate worse than walking in and seeing a little rug in front of a bed.
> **What is that for?** You don't get off the bed in the front. **You get off the bed on the
> side**, and when your feet hit that floor, you want your feet to hit a carpet. So **you want
> that area rug to hold that whole space — bed AND nightstands.**"*

This does not settle §3 — it is one more voice in the ON camp — but it converts the item from
a taste dispute into a **testable predicate**: does the rug's plan extent contain the bed and
both nightstands? Answerable from a scene dump with no render.

Her general sizing rule is stated differently from §3's and measures a different thing:
**102-152 mm (4-6 in) of rug beyond every furniture piece and all of its legs**, versus §3's
457-610 mm beyond the bed's sides and foot. They are not in conflict; §3 sizes to the bed,
this sizes to whatever the rug is asked to hold. And: **a pad goes under almost every rug**, so
a rug's top surface is never at floor level.

### 8.7 OPEN CASEWORK — §5 GETS ITS FIRST USE-BASED ANSWER
§5 asks what an open shelf bay should contain. Three sources converge, and one of them is
retail rather than residential:

- **An open cell can be justified by a USE**, not only by a look: a Thai designer's wardrobe
  reserves one open compartment with a rail *"for the clothes you will wear the next day"*,
  another for a laundry basket, another as a bag display.
- **A designed open wardrobe is not only clothes.** A stylist lists *"a small framed photo, a
  clock, or some faux flowers… a display of favourite jewelry, a special handbag"*, and the
  **backs of shelves painted in a bold colour**; a merchandising lecturer independently says
  *"don't waste the shelves, create a display area"*, that plants *"soften the whole look"*,
  and that shelf back panels *"create a 3D effect and can be painted or textured."*
- **Density is contested and the two answers are opposite.** A merchandising lecturer: *"don't
  bombard them with merchandise — keep it simple. They're more likely to buy if there's LESS
  merchandise."* An archviz reviewer, of a rendered closet: *"maybe fill it even more — my
  closet's stuffed with stuff. This is valuable real estate, you got to fill this up."*
  **A SHOP is merchandised sparse; a HOME closet is full.** Contents styled to shop density
  read as a display rather than as somebody's wardrobe — which is a testable hypothesis for
  the "unnatural contents" verdict, not a resolved rule.

Ordering rules that ARE agreed (a stylist with 20 years of luxury clients): **all garments
face the same direction**; **one hanger colour family**, with the shoulder profile changing by
garment class; **colour runs light-to-dark, restarted at each new category**; short and long
garments get separate zones.

### 8.8 THE FOLD MODULE — A FOLDED STACK HAS A DERIVABLE FOOTPRINT [1SRC]

Source: `icCL89J6w54` — FittDesign Studio, *"How To Professionally Fold Your Garments For
Retail Display"*, 11 min 53 s; watched 2026-08-28, unit
`knowledge/_inbox/video-study/2026-08-28-closet-dimensions-from-a-closet-designer-s9dEdHgCP-Y.md`
§3. **This is a garment maker folding for RETAIL DISPLAY, not a home.** One source.

§5 and §8.7 both ask what an open shelf bay should CONTAIN; neither gives the STACK itself a
module. So on our shelves a folded item's footprint is whatever the mesh happened to be — and
**a pile of unequal footprints cannot read as a stack.** This source is geometric and states
the procedure `[02:15]`–`[04:01]`, for a t-shirt:

1. *"you'll need a **flat surface**… lay your t-shirt **front side down**, make sure it's
   completely flat."*
2. *"fold the **bottom hemline** — we'll go about **a sixth or seventh of the way** — and fold
   it in… **this is done to shorten the overall length.**"*
3. *"use our **fifth lines**: if you look at your t-shirt as if it's spread into fifths — one,
   two, three, four, five — we'll **fold along the side of each fifth fold line**… making sure
   that both of these sections are **equal**."*
4. *"then you'll **fold it in half**."*

**The module — arithmetic on the fractions he states, no dimension is spoken in the video:**

| quantity | derivation | value |
|---|---|---|
| folded WIDTH | outer fifths folded in, three fifths remain | **≈ 3/5 = 0.60 × laid-flat garment width** |
| folded LENGTH | hem turned 1/6–1/7, then halved: (1 − 1/6)/2 … (1 − 1/7)/2 | **≈ 0.42 × garment length** |

Metric-first note: the rule is a RATIO, not a dimension — it yields mm only once a garment's
laid-flat width and length are asserted at ingest (R8 scale assertion). Nothing here licenses a
mm number on its own.

**The equidistant rule, and its named failure.** `[02:50]` *"if they're not equal, what's going
to happen is you're going to get a **wonky t-shirt**, and any graphics you have on the front
will appear **off-centre**."* `[03:24]` *"make sure that both of your fold lines are
**equidistant** — the same distance and the same size."* The defect is not untidiness: the
FACE of the folded piece goes off-axis and whatever sits on it stops being centred. That is a
§8.3-register named defect available for a shelf item, and it is checkable on the mesh —
equal fifths or not — before any render.

**Why a stack is read as a signal at all** `[00:34]`: *"**how you present your clothes on your
racks and on your shelves is going to be an indication of how your brand is going to
resonate**… a good presented store will go a long way towards pushing this exclusive and
premium look."* Same register as the standing owner verdict on our built-in contents
(`ORD-2026-08-26`, *"ของในตู้ built-in ยังไม่เป็นธรรมชาติ"*): the stack is read as care, and
equal square edges with a repeating value step down the pile are what carry the reading.

**WHAT THIS DOES NOT SAY, and it is the same fence as §8.7's density contradiction.** It gives
the MODULE and the EQUIDISTANT rule. It does not say **how many stacks belong in a cell**, nor
**how full a HOME shelf is** — a shop is merchandised sparse and §8.7 records an archviz
reviewer saying a home closet is stuffed. That gap stays open as **VG-01** and this unit does
not close it. Do not let the retail provenance travel any further than the footprint.

### 9.3 A RUN OF BAYS IS GENERATED, NOT FILLED — and this is the answer to ORD-2026-08-26 [1SRC, performed]

`Gh6xKtB0SLA` styles a four-bay run on camera and states a rule that produces the whole run.
**Every other shelf source in this file — §5, §8.7, §8.8 — governs ONE bay or ONE stack.** The
owner's standing verdict is that our built-in's contents read unnatural, and our nine cells are
each composed the same way; nothing on disk could have said that was the defect.

1. **Start at the bay nearest the VIEWER'S eye height** `[07:25]`: *"Start with the shelf that is
   closest to eye level… I'm kind of short. 5'4."* — she names her own stature (1626 mm) as the
   datum. **For us the datum is the hero camera's eye height, and it is already a number in the
   spec.**
2. **Finish that one bay alone, ignoring the rest** `[08:24]`: *"Just set up how you want your
   first shelf to look and only focus on it. Don't worry about the rest of it."*
3. **Compose it as a V — mass at the two ends, the VOID DELIBERATELY IN THE CENTRE** `[08:00]`:
   *"mimics an upside down triangle or a V… we've got an open space right here in the center."*
4. **Then each neighbouring bay INVERTS its neighbour: mass lands where the void was** `[09:28]`.
   Sequence built on camera: **Λ · V · Λ · V**.
5. **The payoff is a viewer response, not a room property** `[09:19]`: *"very satisfying for your
   eye to see that rhythm, but the opposing rhythm of a triangle going down and a triangle going
   up."*
6. **Stated scale-independent, and extended to built-ins by name** `[09:57]`: *"this works no
   matter what… on bookshelves, on shelves that are built into your home."*

> **THE VOID IS AN AUTHORED, PLACED ELEMENT WITH A LOCATION.** §5 says *≥25% of every shelf
> surface empty* and §1 says 60/40 — both are AREA budgets with no position. A bay can satisfy
> either while putting its emptiness where nothing reads. Checkable on our scene dump per bay:
> where is the centroid of the occupied mass, and does it alternate down the run.

**And a second practitioner licenses REPETITION of a whole composition, under two conditions:**
*"making the same styled shelf on different sides of the bookcases at different heights… you're
only styling half the shelves and just duplicating the other half"* — `AWWPnKYNklw` `[00:53]`.
Different SIDE and different HEIGHT, both required. A professional duplicates deliberately; we
duplicate by accident and it reads as a grid.

### 9.4 THE BAY'S INTERNAL GRAMMAR — three roles, three columns, three book modes

- **THREE ROLES, filled in order, before any height is considered** — `Gh6xKtB0SLA` `[01:09]`:
  **HERO** (*"what specific item… draws your attention first"*) → **GROUNDING** (risers, book
  stacks — *"pieces that either corral other smaller accessories or they elevate accessories"*
  `[03:13]`) → **SUPPORTING** (small personality pieces). §1's triangle orders things already
  chosen by HEIGHT; this is a SELECTION taxonomy that runs first, and every bay must carry all
  three.
- **HEROES PER RUN ≈ one per 1.3 bays** — `Gh6xKtB0SLA` `[02:22]` names three hero pieces across
  four bays (a large artwork at eye level, a second artwork lower, a dried arrangement). An
  un-heroed bay is allowed; a bay that is all hero is not. First count of focal objects below
  room scale anywhere in this file.
- **THREE COLUMNS PER BOARD** — `K9V0cslPa6I` `[03:34]`: *"divide them into three… your left, your
  center, and your right. And this imaginary little grid will do wonders… as opposed to having
  everything just crammed in the middle."* **This supplies the denominator §5's X-pattern is
  missing** — "never stacked in the same column" was uncheckable because no column was defined.
- **THREE BOOK MODES, ALL THREE PRESENT** — `K9V0cslPa6I` `[05:32]`: horizontal pile · vertical
  run · **tilted lean**, and *"ideally you will use all three different ways… so that it doesn't
  look stale and repetitive."* Countable predicate on our mesh: `modes_present == 3`.
- **BOOKS ARE PLACED BEFORE THE LARGEST OBJECTS** — `AWWPnKYNklw` `[01:31]`: *"after the books are
  in place I always start with my biggest items first."* Books are substrate, not competitors.
  Six passes by CLASS, not by size: books → biggest → stackable trinket boxes → plants → a 2D
  element → smalls peppered last `[01:45]`.
- **EVERY BAY NEEDS A 2D ELEMENT among the 3D objects** — `AWWPnKYNklw` `[02:27]`: a leaned
  painting or framed photo, *"it just keeps things from being to one note."* Checkable predicate
  per cell: at least one planar item.
- **A HORIZONTAL BOOK STACK NEEDS CLEARANCE ON BOTH SIDES** — `K9V0cslPa6I` `[06:28]`: it may not
  be wedged between vertical runs. An adjacency rule, which no area budget can express.

### 9.5 ELEVATION IS THE SECOND REMEDY FOR "TOO SMALL", AND IT IS THE CHEAP ONE
