## 1. The diagnosis this file is really about

A flat plane meant to be high-end millwork reads as a floor when the texture
misrepresents the **scale, orientation, symmetry or edge detailing** of real
cabinet construction. The tells below are ordered by how cheaply each is fixed in
a render, not by how often they occur.

## 2. Leaf width and panel geometry — the structural signature

Fine cabinetry is **not tiled with narrow random planks.** A panel is spliced from
thin *leaves*. Plain-sliced leaves run roughly **152 mm to 305 mm** wide (6–12 in).
Architectural practice calls for a panel to be **center-balanced**: an even number
of equal-width leaves, centred across the face.

| panel width | leaves to render |
|---|---|
| 1000 mm | 4, 6 or 8 equal leaves (e.g. 6 × 166.7 mm) |
| 2000 mm | 8, 10 or 12 equal leaves |

Flooring, by contrast, uses **offset units of mixed or random widths**, never
symmetrically balanced about a 1–2 m frame. A veneer map with no leaf structure at
all — a stretched floor texture — has none of this signature and cannot acquire it
by tuning.

## 3. The mirror seam, and the optical effect that proves it

**Book matching** flips every other leaf like the pages of a book, producing a
biological mirror at the joint and symmetrical diamond or heart figures. Because
the rift edges are joined, the physical seam is nearly invisible.

Flipping alternate leaves also produces the **barber pole** effect: the slicing
blade compresses one face (*tight side*) and slightly fractures the other (*loose
side*), so alternating leaves take light differently and the panel shows
**alternating light and dark vertical bands that invert as the view or light angle
moves.** Floor planks are laid slip-matched, tight-side up, for uniform wear — they
have no such anisotropy. This is the cue that most directly separates the two, and
it is the one our own frames have never carried.

**Flitch sequencing.** In a continuous run of cabinetry, consecutive leaves from one
log are kept in order, so a mineral streak or a sweep in the grain transitions
gradually across adjacent panels. Flooring deliberately scatters unsequenced boards
from different trees. A repeating tile is therefore wrong twice over: it is neither
sequenced nor random.

## 4. The two betrayals that are pure geometry

- **Staggered butt joints.** Flooring uses short boards (≈300–2200 mm) meeting at
  randomised end joints. Cabinet panels use continuous leaves running the full
  height of the door (to 2.4–3.0 m). **A horizontal or offset seam across a cabinet
  face reads as floor immediately.**
- **Micro-bevels.** Pre-finished flooring carries a **1–2 mm** V-groove around every
  plank to disguise subfloor lippage. Cabinet panels are spliced flush and sanded
  to one plane. If a render puts **linear micro-shadows at the joint lines**, the
  continuous reflection of the cabinet face breaks and the eye reads plank
  boundaries.

## 5. Triage against a live build — APPLIED, REFUSED, OUTSTANDING

Recorded because a distillation that drops what its own studio measured is worth
less than no distillation.

- **APPLIED — micro-bevel relief.** A build was running the floor's normal map at
  full default strength on cabinet veneer, because the relief strength was keyed by
  map SLUG and every veneer shared the floor's slug. One parameter carrying two
  things. Veneer relief is now a small fraction of the floor's, pinned by a test as
  a RELATION so the two cannot be collapsed again.
- **REFUTED — "cathedral figure must run strictly vertically."** The source states
  horizontal cathedral arches are *"anatomically impossible for a continuous trunk
  cut."* A measurement of the artefact being reproduced returned **2.26**
  horizontal-to-vertical grain energy on its altar face, and the map aspect was set
  to match. **The measurement wins.** The claim is also scoped narrower than it
  reads: it describes tall door panels, and grain running horizontally along a long
  low drawer front is ordinary work. Do not apply the vertical rule as a law.
- **OUTSTANDING — leaf count, centre-balancing, barber pole.** §2 and §3 are
  specified well enough to build (generate leaves at a measured pitch, mirror
  alternate leaves, centre the set) and are **not built**. Barber-pole banding is
  testable directly against a target photograph and has **not been measured**.

### 7.1 การจัดแผ่น 6 แบบ / Six named matches — and ours has a name

| Match | What it is |
|---|---|
| **BOOK** | *"the most common way that veneer is matched"* — alternate leaves flipped, *"flip one open like it's the pages of a book"*; the sapwood lands in the centre and the seam is a MIRROR |
| **SEQUENCE** | sapwood cut off first, leaves arranged *"so that they appear the most uniform in colour and pattern"* |
| **SLIP** | *"simply sliding one sheet of veneer alongside of the other"* — translated, **not** flipped, so there is no mirror |
| **END** | leaves flipped along their ends *"making it look like one longer piece continuing the pattern"* |
| **RANDOM** | *"no rhyme or reason"* — some book, some slip; reads rustic |
| **PLANK** | like random, but *"the pieces have different widths"* |

Orthogonal to all six: **BALANCE MATCH** — the leaves are *"each spaced evenly"*, and the
test is stated as a thing you look for: *"there's not a skinny stripe at this end or a skinny
stripe at this end."*

> **This is a predicate a build can be checked against without a render**: if the end boards
> of a laid-up wall are a fraction of a full board width, the wall is not balance-matched and
> a joiner would not have built it that way. It is the same shape as the 32 mm system's
> equal-setback rule in `knowledge/ergonomics/casework-fixture-clearances-th-practice.md`
> §8.3 — **two trades, two subjects, one rule: the spacing at the two ends must match, and it
> is judged by eye.**

### 7.2 ทำไมผนังจริงถึง "เกือบเหมือน" ไม่ใช่ "เหมือนเป๊ะ" / Why a real laid-up wall drifts

Leaves are sliced **1/32 to 1/40 inch (0.79-0.64 mm)** thick and *"they're kept in the same
order as they came in the tree"*, so:

> *"any pattern that's in any given piece is going to continue to occur in the next piece —
> it's going to look **almost** the same as the previous piece, and so on through the line. As
> you get down further in the stack the pattern might change a bit more, but any two leaves
> that are **consecutive** are going to have something that looks pretty similar."*

The worked example on camera: *"this little eye here has become a bit smaller here, yet the
overall pattern is still pretty much the same."*

**Therefore a real wall is neither an exact repeat nor random. It is a CONTROLLED DRIFT:
adjacent boards nearly alike, changing monotonically along the run.** An exact repeat and an
exact mirror are two states a tree cannot produce.

This is the one place the general archviz rule inverts (see
`knowledge/rendering/practitioner-consensus-eye-light-camera.md` §3.1): free per-tile random
rotation is right for scattered props and ground textures and **wrong** for boards laid up
from sequential leaves.

Two more facts from the same layup, both R10-class:
- **A panel must be veneered on BOTH faces.** *"the reason we have to veneer both sides of a
  panel is because it keeps it balanced — if you only veneered one side you would actually
  cause the panel to bend, and that's due to the glue drying and pulling on one side."* A
  backing veneer *"with similar characteristics"* is used even where it will never be seen.
- **A book match on straight grain is deliberately angled.** *"since this is a pretty
  straight-grained wood, if we just simply open it up like this it isn't quite as
  interesting, so what we're going to do is angle it just a little bit so it kind of makes a
  V-shape"* — and the joiner chooses whether the V points up or down.

### 7.3 การจัดหาแผ่น / Procurement — drift is managed, not left to chance
*"just because pieces of wood or veneer are cut from the same species of tree does not
necessarily mean they're going to have the same appearance… these two pieces of white oak,
they are very different in colour. So you need to make sure that you request **sequenced
panels** if more than one panel is needed for a particular project."* Leaves are **numbered**
on receipt, because the sequence is the material.

### 7.4 ช่องว่างที่ตัดสินว่าจะใช้ตัวสร้างแบบไหน / THE GAP THAT DECIDES THE GENERATOR

Everything in §7.1-§7.3 assumes the wall is **veneered**. A Thai builder source states that
the common Thai built-in is **factory melamine-faced board** (MDF, or moisture-resistant HMR
board, which is dyed **green** for identification), chosen precisely because it is cut,
bored, edge-banded and finished in the factory and only **assembled** on site — which removes
the skilled site labour that carries the risk of a contractor abandoning the job. HPL
(*"โฟเมก้า"* is a brand; the material is High Pressure Laminate) and painted solid wood both
require that skilled labour.

**If a wall is melamine or HPL rather than veneer, its repetition is a PRINTED DÉCOR with a
fixed repeat length, not a flitch drifting leaf to leaf — and the correct fix is the décor's
repeat and rotation, not book-versus-slip matching.** The two answers imply different
generators.

**This file cannot close that.** Which material a given wall is meant to be is a
specification question for the project and, for a Thai job, a constructability question for
the owner or his fabricator. It is recorded here so that nobody applies §7.1-§7.2 to a
melamine wall and gets a confident wrong answer.

It also sits in direct tension with the honest-materials position held by some designers (no
surface printed to imitate another material), which the Thai market's default built-in
surface violates by construction. Both are true; the project must choose, not this file.

### 8.1 Where a real run legitimately breaks — and one unverified bundle count

Sequentially sliced leaves are kept in slicing order, **commonly** in quantities
of **24 to 32 leaves per bundle** — a figure whose ONLY citation in the DR is the
synthesised self-source (*Tectonic and Computational Frameworks...*); no trade
document in the corpus states it, so treat it as unverified. Separately: because
wood is organic the grain changes subtly leaf to leaf (bare marker [3] in the
staged unit; no source title printed), but the sequence lets a fabricator
reassemble them with *"only a very gradual transition in grain and figure across
adjacent leaves"* — and THAT quotation is the part carrying trade citations
(*3.2 Material - Architectural Woodwork Institute*; *vt industries / VENEER
GUIDELINES*). Across consecutive panels a prominent characteristic will
**ascend or descend** as the leaves progress (*Surfaces - Oregon Door*).

**The DR also seconds §3's barber pole, on a trade citation §3 did not have.**
Because the *tight* and *loose* sides of sliced veneer alternate in a book
match, they reflect light and absorb finishes differently, giving an alternating
light-and-dark striped **barber pole** optical effect (*Ansi Wdma - Is 1a 2013
Flush*, co-signed by the synthesised report) — a second, trade-tier source for a
cause §3 held on one. The same block gives slip match a *"leaning/sloping
appearance on tall surfaces"* (*A Pacific Northwest Company - Cloudfront.net*)
and random match leaves *"often from different logs"* laid with *"a conscious
effort to mismatch the grain"*. None of it measures the banding — see §8.5.

§7.2's CONTROLLED DRIFT stays QUALITATIVE here: the DR adds trade wording for it
(a *"very gradual transition"*, characteristics that ascend or descend) and one
unverified count, not an amplitude — no leaf-to-leaf drift amplitude is stated
anywhere in the DR, and §8.6 records the refusal. **OUR READING, NOT THE
SOURCE'S:** if a bundle commonly holds 24-32 leaves, a run longer than that
cannot come from one bundle, so a long wall would contain at least one honest
break. The premise that has to hold for that to follow — that a flitch is one
bundle — is **NOT IN SOURCES**, the number itself is unverified (above), and the
DR states no leaf-count ceiling for a continuous run. What the DR actually
requires is narrower and is a FLITCH condition, not a bundle count: a break is
required when one flitch cannot sequence-match the room, and it must then land at
an opening or a corner (below).

**Where the pattern legitimately breaks:**

- **Flitch transitions.** When one flitch cannot sequence-match a whole room, the
  transition must be placed **at a wall opening or a change of plane (a corner)**,
  to naturally interrupt and reset the grain pattern (*3.4 Aesthetic - AWI*,
  co-signed by the synthesised report). Our reading, not the source's: the reset
  then reads as architecture rather than as an error.
- **Pre-manufactured sets** typically consist of **6 to 12 panels** (*3.4
  Aesthetic - AWI*), and **sequence matching between independent sets is not
  required.**
- **Layup symmetry tolerance.** Made-to-order sets require balance matching
  (uniform leaf width) or centre-balance matching (an even number of uniform
  leaves with a centre joint). At the outer edges the flitch width may differ by
  up to **1 inch (25.4 mm)** in Custom layouts and **1/2 inch (12.7 mm)** in
  Premium (*A Pacific Northwest Company - Cloudfront.net*, co-signed by the
  synthesised report — **NOT an AWI figure**).
- **End-match misalignment at panel joints** must not exceed **4.8 mm [0.188 in]**
  Premium or **9.5 mm [0.375 in]** Custom (*3.4 Aesthetic - AWI*).

> Only ONE of these rows speaks to §7.1's balance-match predicate, and it does
> not say what the predicate says. At the outer edges the **flitch width** may
> differ by up to 12.7 mm in Premium / 25.4 mm in Custom (*A Pacific Northwest
> Company*, not AWI). Whether that licenses a narrower END LEAF than the field
> leaves — which is what §7.1's skinny-stripe test is about — is NOT stated;
> reading it that way is our interpretation. The end-match misalignment row
> (4.8 / 9.5 mm) measures **joint misalignment between panels**, not end-stripe
> width, and is not a balance-match number at all.

### 8.3 Randomisation operators — and the one that is ours

Blender is the only row here that is directly actionable; the V-Ray, Corona and
Unreal numbers are recorded as **TEST STARTING POINTS**, never thresholds of
record, under the same law as the Corona-card caveat in `texture-sets-discord`
§5.3.

**Blender shader nodes (the buildable recipe):**
- Drive per-board variation from the Geometry node's **Random Per Island** output
  or the Object Info node's **Random** value, passed through a 1D or 2D White
  Noise Texture node to generate unique coordinate shift vectors.
- **A 180-degree flip, or a scale inversion on the X or Y axis,** is achieved by
  running a White Noise 2D through a Math (Greater Than) node as a **0 or 1**
  boolean gate, converted to a scale factor of **-1 or 1** by Math (Multiply
  Add), driving a Mapping node.
- **THE TRAP:** when Geometry Nodes instances are realised (a Realize Instances
  node, **as used for** boolean operations such as a window cutout), **the
  Object Info random parameter is destroyed.** To survive realisation, assign a
  custom attribute on a **0 to 1** range to each instance inside Geometry Nodes;
  it stays readable in the Shader Editor afterwards.
- Blender **UV offset jitter**, exact scale-jitter percentages, and direct
  hue/saturation/value node ranges are **NOT IN SOURCES** — as are Blender-native
  jitter amplitude ranges generally (the DR's own caveat paragraph).

**Ported starting points from other engines, marked as such:**
- **A rotation step of 180 degrees is COMMONLY SPECIFIED for directional wood**
  — it reverses grain direction while keeping the grain axis. (Corona UVW
  Randomizer; 180 here is an ANGLE.) V-Ray's rotation jitter spans **0 to 360
  degrees**; the DR says *"'Steps' define the step angle"*, but its own worked
  example — a rotation step of **5** restricting rotation to multiples of
  **72 degrees** — can only be a COUNT (**OUR READING, NOT THE SOURCE'S**: the
  DR's definition and its example contradict each other). Rotation may be locked
  0-to-0 to hold strict parallel rows.
- **Corona Multi Shader accepts up to 100 inputs and provides HUE and GAMMA
  randomisation** (bare markers [67, 68] in the staged unit; no source title
  printed — the DR's caveat list names Corona docs among its co-cites generally,
  but not for this row). Separately, and from a different citation: **in TILE
  setups** a value of around **2.5%** for hue, saturation and gamma random
  offsets breaks visual repetition without an unnatural colour shift — its only
  named human source is a self-described beginner's *r/3dsmax* thread, co-signed
  by the synthesised report, which makes it the weakest citation in this section;
  the saturation channel comes from that thread, not from the Multi Shader row
  above. V-Ray's demonstrated VRayEnmesh values are Hue 5, Saturation 3, Gamma 8
  on its own scale.
- Bertrand Benoit's Floor Generator **default layout setup** uses a **slight**
  per-board **gamma variation of 0.2** (to make some boards subtly darker). In
  the same CGSource MultiTexture / Floor Generator block, but NOT tied to that
  default setup, the DR also gives a **scale variation of 30%** and a tilt
  parameter applying micro-rotations so boards catch highlights individually.
- V-Ray UV offset jitter is allowed over **0 to 1** (up to a full tile) and scale
  jitter over **10% to 150%**.
- Unreal macro variation multiplies **3 samples of a macro variation texture** at
  **3 different coordinates** (*Landscapes - Material Concepts |
  Unrealcode.net*). Cell bombing costs **4 texture lookups per layer** (7 with
  displacement) (*Texture Bombing - Epic Developer* documentation), has what the
  DR calls a **good** range for its min/max scale parameters, **0.9 to 1.1** (a
  single *Hoj Dee* tutorial — not documentary, and materially weaker than the
  Epic row above it), and expands the cell texture's R and G to a -1..1 range by
  multiplying by 2 and subtracting 1.
  **The DR states the technique's own limit** (bare marker [96] in the staged
  unit; no source title printed): cell bombing suits organic textures and can
  disrupt uniform layouts like brick walls or tiled wood floors.

> **THE CONTRADICTION THIS SECTION MUST NOT HIDE, because applying it blindly
> would undo §7.2.** Everything in the list above is anti-tiling machinery built
> for FLOORS and scattered surfaces. §7.2 says a laid-up veneer wall is neither an
> exact repeat nor random but a **controlled drift**, and
> `knowledge/rendering/practitioner-consensus-eye-light-camera.md` §3.1 records
> the same inversion. **Free per-board rotation and colour jitter are therefore
> WRONG for a book-matched wall and right for a floor.** What legitimately
> transfers to the wall is narrow and named: 4-8 distinct leaf maps ordered as a
> monotone sequence, the MIRROR on alternate leaves — a scale inversion of -1 on
> the across-grain axis, which is what a book match physically is (NOT the
> 180-degree rotation step above, which is Corona's directional-wood recipe) —
> and a jitter amplitude small enough to read as leaf-to-leaf drift rather than
> as scatter. Everything else in the list belongs to the floor.

  joinery of veneer-faced paneling and MDF to be fully **"Mitered"**, factory
  prepared and machined for visual uniformity (*3.3 Structural - AWI*; *3.4
  Aesthetic - AWI*).

**Arris and chamfer:**
- An arris is the sharp edge where two surfaces meet (*Glossary Archive -
  Architectural Woodwork Institute*).
- In physical woodwork sharp arris lines are **not permitted** — they are
  vulnerable to impact damage and fail to hold finish (**synthesised report only;
  no trade document in the corpus states the prohibition**).
- Veneer edges are traditionally **"eased"** by lightly striking the sharp edge
  with a fine abrasive, or machined to a small radius (*Glossary of Terms -
  Leininger Cabinet & Woodworking*; *Ansi Wdma - Is 1a 2013 Flush*) — **the one
  claim in this group with independent trade backing**, and better sourced than
  the prohibition above it.
- Manual edge-banding trimmers apply bevel blades at chamfer angles of
  **45 degrees and 60 degrees** (from two hand-tool product listings — blade
  angles offered by trimmer manufacturers, **not** a specified millwork chamfer
  standard).

> **The conclusion below rests on the EASED bullet, which is trade-cited, not on
> the prohibition, which is not.** A veneer panel modelled with a perfectly
> sharp, unrelieved arris is not a simplification of real millwork: the trade
> eases that edge, and the synthesised report records it as a state the trade
> forbids. **It is not an open defect on our BUILT geometry** — `build_room.py`
> bevels the meshes it builds, but the widths are not one number: the suite path
> (the P2 frame of record) runs `_bevel_edges(width_m=0.005)`, a **5 mm**
> round-over, with millwork parts overriding to `MILL_BEVEL_M = 0.0012`, while
> only the bare-rect path uses the `BEVEL_WIDTH_M = 0.001` default. It also
> **skips imported `ph_model` meshes by construction**, so acquired meshes are
> in the render path and unbevelled. Both the acquired meshes and the 5 mm suite
> default are unaddressed here — the function's own docstring says of that pass
> that *"a 5 mm round-over on a cabinet door is not a thing that exists."* What
> §8.4 adds is trade backing for the PRACTICE of relieving that edge, not for
> any particular width: the DR gives none (*"machined to a small radius"*,
> unquantified, and the only numbers in the group are hand-tool blade angles).
> The DR says nothing about our geometry either way. OUR render-side inference,
> not in the source: the eased edge is what gives a panel edge a highlight to
> catch.
